# Learning Path: How to Start Tai Chi and Qigong

> **TL;DR** — There is no wrong door. **Start with qigong** (gentler, simpler)
> or **tai chi** (more detailed), practice **10–20 minutes most days** rather
> than long sessions, and progress in clear stages: **feel the breath → stand
> and root → learn a short form → deepen the principles**. No equipment, no
> belief system, and it adapts to any age or fitness level.

One of the most common questions beginners ask is not "what is tai chi?" but
"*how do I actually start, and what comes next?*" Most sites answer with a
definition and leave you to figure out the sequence. This page is the missing
map: a plain, staged path from your first week to years of practice.

---

## The path at a glance

| Stage | What you learn | Typical timeframe | Sign you're ready to move on |
|-------|----------------|-------------------|------------------------------|
| **1. Feel the breath** | Slow abdominal breathing | First 1–2 weeks | You can slow your exhale without strain |
| **2. Stand and root** | Zhan Zhuang standing, posture, sinking | Weeks 2–6 | You can stand relaxed for 5+ minutes |
| **3. Learn a short form** | Baduanjin or the Tai Chi 24-form | Months 1–6 | You can move through it from memory |
| **4. Deepen the principles** | Song, rooting, yin–yang, internal work | Months 6+ | Movement feels soft, continuous, grounded |
| **5. Refine for years** | Push hands, silk reeling, meditation | Years | Practice is a daily, self-sustaining habit |

---

## Stage 1 — Feel the breath

Everything in qigong and tai chi rests on the breath, so this is where you
begin — before movement, before postures.

- **Learn slow abdominal breathing.** Inhale gently through the nose, let the
  belly relax and expand, and exhale slowly through the nose or mouth.
- **Aim to lengthen the exhale**, not to force anything. A comfortable, slow
  exhale is the whole skill at this stage.
- **Practice 5 minutes a day**, seated or lying down is fine to start.

Read [Qigong Breathing](articles/qigong-breathing.md) for the full method.
The goal is simply to make slow, low-in-the-body breathing feel natural.

---

## Stage 2 — Stand and root

Once the breath is comfortable, add standing. This is the physical foundation
for everything that follows.

- **Learn Zhan Zhuang (站樁, *zhànzhuāng*)**, "standing like a post" — the
  simplest and most important posture in the whole system.
- **Key points:** feet shoulder-width, knees soft, tailbone gently tucked,
  shoulders and chest released, weight sinking through the feet.
- **Build slowly:** one minute, then three, then five, over weeks — never
  forcing, never locking the knees.

Read [Zhan Zhuang](articles/zhan-zhuang.md) and
[Rooting](articles/rooting.md). Standing teaches the qualities — softness,
sinking, stability — that make the moving forms work.

---

## Stage 3 — Learn a short form

Now add movement. Choose one short sequence and learn it well rather than
collecting many half-learned routines.

- **Qigong route:** start with [Baduanjin](articles/baduanjin.md) (the
  Eight Pieces of Brocade) — the world's most practiced qigong routine.
- **Tai chi route:** start with the [Tai Chi 24-form](articles/tai-chi-24-form.md),
  the simplified Yang-style sequence most people learn first.
- **Work one posture at a time**, chaining what you know into a slow,
  continuous loop. Correctness and relaxation before speed.

A teacher or a good video course is genuinely useful here — alignment is hard
to see from the inside.

---

## Stage 4 — Deepen the principles

Once a form is memorized, the real study begins. This is where tai chi stops
being "slow exercise" and becomes an internal practice.

- **Song (鬆, relaxed release)** — the foundational quality of soft, open,
  tension-free structure. Read [Song](articles/song.md).
- **The three regulations** — body, breath, and mind working as one. Read
  [Qigong Principles](articles/qigong-principles.md).
- **Yin and yang in movement** — empty/full, open/close, the alternation
  behind every step. Read [Yin and Yang](articles/yin-yang.md).

This stage has no endpoint — it is the ongoing refinement that defines the
practice.

---

## Stage 5 — Refine for years

For those who want to go further, the deeper material:

- **Silk reeling (纏絲勁, *chánsījìn*)** — the spiral power of tai chi. Read
  [Silk Reeling](articles/silk-reeling.md).
- **Push hands (推手, *tuīshǒu*)** — the partner practice that tests your
  structure. Read [Push Hands](articles/push-hands.md).
- **Neigong (內功)** — the internal work beneath the movement. Read
  [Neigong](articles/neigong.md).

None of these are required. They are the long road, for people who find that
the practice has become part of their life.

## Who should be cautious

Tai chi and qigong are gentle and low-impact, but a responsible note:

- If you have a **balance, joint, cardiovascular, or respiratory condition**,
  check with a healthcare professional before starting.
- **Never force a stretch or a posture.** Work at a comfortable edge; pain is
  a signal to back off.
- You can do nearly everything **seated** or with support if standing is
  difficult.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I learn tai chi or qigong first?**
For most beginners, qigong is the gentler on-ramp — it is slower, simpler, and
easier to learn correctly from a video or class. Tai chi is a longer, more
detailed study. The two share the same foundation, so either order works; many
people start with qigong and add tai chi once they have basic body awareness.

**How much should I practice each day?**
Consistency beats length. Ten to twenty minutes most days is far more
effective than an occasional two-hour session. Even five minutes of standing
or slow breathing daily builds a habit and a baseline.

**Do I need a teacher, or can I learn online?**
A qualified teacher is the best way to catch alignment errors early, but a
good video course plus careful self-practice is a legitimate start. If you
have any health condition, consult a professional before beginning.

**How long until I see results?**
People often notice feeling calmer and more mobile within a few weeks of
regular practice. Deeper changes — better balance, posture, body awareness —
build over months and years. Progress is gradual and cumulative, not linear.

**What equipment do I need?**
None. Loose, comfortable clothing and a flat, non-slip surface are enough.
Flat-soled shoes or bare feet (indoors) are typical; you do not need a mat or
special gear.

**Can I start if I am older or not flexible?**
Yes. Tai chi and qigong are among the most adaptable practices there are.
Nearly every movement can be done smaller, higher, seated, or with support.
You start from wherever you are, not from an ideal.

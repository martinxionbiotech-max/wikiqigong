# Medical Disclaimer

**WikiQigong is an educational resource. It is not medical advice.**

The content on this site is provided for general educational and informational
purposes only. It describes traditional Chinese mind–body practices — tai chi,
qigong, meditation, and breathing — and their historical and cultural context.
It does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease, and it does not
replace care from a qualified healthcare professional.

## What this site is not

- We are **not** providing medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment.
- We are **not** recommending any practice as a substitute for professional
  medical care, medication, physical therapy, or emergency services.
- We are **not** claiming that any practice described here cures, treats,
  prevents, or reverses any disease or health condition.
- We are **not** your clinician, therapist, physiotherapist, or exercise
  instructor. Reading this site does not create a professional relationship.

## Who this information is for

The articles are written for a general English-speaking reader who wants to
understand tai chi, qigong, and Chinese mind–body concepts. They assume no
medical training and no prior practice. They are not a clinical protocol, a
rehabilitation plan, a substitute for a supervised class, or a prescription for
a particular person.

If you are a patient, a caregiver, or a practitioner working with a clinical
population, use this material as background reading and follow the instructions
of the professionals who know the person and the situation.

## Health wording and evidence

Where research is discussed, we describe findings cautiously and with their
actual strength of evidence. Terms like *"may support,"* *"may help,"* and
*"has been studied for"* are used deliberately. They are not marketing
language; they reflect the current, often preliminary state of the evidence
base for mind–body practices.

We distinguish three kinds of statement:

1. **Historical and cultural context** — what a tradition says, when a text was
   compiled, and how a term has been used. This is not a medical claim.
2. **Traditional interpretation** — a framework such as qi, yin–yang, or the
   dantian. It can be meaningful within the tradition without being a
   biomedical measurement.
3. **Modern research** — studies of tai chi or qigong for a defined outcome.
   The result depends on the population, the protocol, the comparator, and the
   quality of the study. A single trial does not establish a treatment.

A statement that a practice "may support balance" is not a promise that it will
prevent a fall, and it is not a reason to stop prescribed medication or
supervision.

## Individual variation

People differ in mobility, medical history, medication, balance, breathing
capacity, sensory sensitivity, and prior experience. A practice that is
comfortable for one reader may be unsuitable for another. The site cannot know
your condition, your environment, or the quality of your instruction.

Do not use a general article to decide whether a specific movement is safe for
you. Ask a qualified professional who can assess you in person.

## Before you practice

- If you have a medical condition — especially cardiovascular, respiratory,
  balance-related, musculoskeletal, neurological, or metabolic — consult a
  qualified healthcare professional before beginning any new movement or
  breathing practice.
- If you are pregnant, consult your care provider before beginning or changing
  a practice.
- If you have recently had surgery, an injury, a fall, or a period of
  immobilisation, get clearance before loading the affected area.
- If you take medication that affects blood pressure, heart rate, balance, or
  breathing, ask your prescriber whether the practice needs to be modified.
- If you are older, practice near a stable support and avoid exposed edges,
  slippery floors, and unlit spaces.
- If you are teaching or supervising others, use your own qualification and
  insurance, not this website, as the basis for instruction.

## Stop signs during practice

Stop and seek appropriate help if you experience:

- chest pain, pressure, or tightness;
- fainting, near-fainting, or sudden severe dizziness;
- unusual shortness of breath, wheezing, or a racing heartbeat;
- new weakness, numbness, or loss of balance;
- sharp joint pain, or pain that worsens as you continue;
- a headache, visual change, or confusion that is new to you.

Do not try to "push through" a symptom to complete a sequence. A practice can be
modified, shortened, or stopped. If symptoms are severe or sudden, contact
emergency services.

## Breathing and meditation cautions

Breathing exercises are not risk-free for every person. Deliberate slow
breathing, breath holds, or extended meditation can be uncomfortable for people
with panic attacks, trauma histories, dissociative experiences, or certain
respiratory conditions. Stop if a practice produces distress, and seek
qualified support if the reaction is unfamiliar or difficult to manage.

Meditation is not a substitute for mental-health treatment. If you are in
crisis, contact local emergency or crisis services rather than relying on a
website.

supervision of a qualified instructor and a parent or guardian. Do not use the
articles as a standalone programme for a minor.

# How to Pronounce Tai Chi & Qigong Terms

> **TL;DR** — The most common mispronunciations are **qi** (it is *chee*, not
> *key*), **qigong** (*chee-gong*, not *kwee-gong*), and **tai chi** (*tie-jee*,
> not *tie-chee*). This page gives you plain-English pronunciations, the pinyin,
> and the tone marks — so you can say the terms correctly and read our pages
> with confidence.

Chinese terms look intimidating in roman letters. The letters `q`, `x`, and
`zh` do not make the sounds an English speaker expects. This guide is a
cheat sheet: the sound first, the pinyin second, and the tone third.

---

## Why pronunciation matters (and why it is often wrong)

Getting a term right is a small act of respect for the culture the practice
comes from — and it prevents two opposite errors:

- **Mystification** — mangled pronunciation is part of what makes these
  practices feel "exotic" and untouchable.
- **Erasure** — refusing to say the Chinese word at all (and replacing it with
  a loose translation) loses the context.

We use **pinyin with tone marks** because it is precise and traceable. But
every entry also gives a plain-English "say it like" version, because
readability matters too.

---

## The sounds that trip people up

| Pinyin letter | Sound | Say it like |
|---------------|-------|-------------|
| **q** | a soft "ch" between English *ch* and *ts* | the "ch" in "cheese" (but lighter) |
| **x** | a soft "sh" (like *sh* but flatter) | "sh" in "she," tongue forward |
| **zh** | a hard "j" | the "j" in "jam" |
| **c** | a hard "ts" | the "ts" in "cats" |
| **z** | a "dz" | the "ds" in "lads" |
| **e** (after consonants) | a "uh" (like the *u* in "sun") | "uh" |
| **-ong** | "ong" with a nasal *ng* | "gong" |

The single most useful fact: **pinyin `q` is never an English "k" sound.**
That one correction fixes "qi," "qigong," and a dozen related words.

| Yin / Yang | yīn / yáng | **yeen / yahng** | yin flat, yang rising |
| Zhan Zhuang | zhàn zhuāng | **jan jwahng** | "standing post" |
| Baduanjin | bāduànjǐn | **bah-dwahn-jeen** | "eight pieces of brocade" |
| Wuji | wújí | **woo-jee** | "without limit" |
| Song | sōng | **soong** | "relaxed release"; rhymes with "sung" |
| Jing | jīng | **jeeng** | "essence"; high level tone |
| Shen | shén | **shun** (rising) | "spirit"; rising tone |
| Liu Zi Jue | liù zì jué | **lyoh dzuh jweh** | "six healing sounds" |

The "say it like" column is an approximation for English speakers, not a
perfect phonetic transcription. Use it to get close; the pinyin is the
authoritative form.

## The four tones, in one minute

Mandarin has four tones plus a neutral tone. They change meaning, so we mark
them:

| Tone | Mark | Shape | Example | Say it like |
|------|------|-------|---------|-------------|
| 1st | ā | high, level | gōng (功) | a steady high note |
| 2nd | á | rising | qín (禽) | like asking a question |
| 3rd | ǎ | falling then rising | wǔ (五) | a dip down and up |
| 4th | à | falling, sharp | qì (氣) | like a firm command |
| neutral | (none) | light | le (了) | short and unstressed |

You do not need to master tones to practice — but if you are learning the
terms, the tone mark tells you *which* word you are saying. In pinyin, **qì**
(氣, "vital energy") and **qí** (奇, "strange") are different words with
different characters.

## Why we write characters, pinyin, *and* plain English

Every concept on this site appears three ways:

1. **Characters** (氣) — the real thing, so the Chinese is preserved.
2. **Pinyin with tones** (qì) — the precise, pronounceable form.
3. **Plain-English translation** ("breath; air; vital energy") — so a beginner
   understands it in one line.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you actually pronounce 'qi'?**
Chee — like "cheese" without the "se." It is not "key" and not "kwee." The
initial q in pinyin represents a sound between English "ch" and "ts," and qi
(qì) carries a falling tone.

**How do you pronounce 'qigong'?**
Chee-gong — "chee" as in qi, then "gong" like the "gong" in "gong show," with
a nasal "ng" at the end. Both syllables have falling tones: qì gōng.

**How do you pronounce 'tai chi'?**
Tie jee — "tai" rhymes with "tie," and "chi" is like "jee" in "jeep." The full
name tàijíquán is "tie-jee-chwen."

**Why do people write both 'tai chi' and 'taiji'?**
Both transcribe the same characters (太極). "Tai chi" comes from the older
Wade–Giles romanization; "taiji" comes from pinyin, the modern standard. This
site uses pinyin with tone marks for precision.

**Do the tone marks matter?**
Yes, for accuracy — a different tone can be a different word in Mandarin. We
include them for correctness and traceability, but beginners do not need to
master them to practice.

**How do I pronounce 'dantian'?**
Dahn-tyen — "dan" rhymes with "dawn," and "tian" is "tyen," one syllable. The
pinyin is dāntián, with a falling tone on the second syllable.

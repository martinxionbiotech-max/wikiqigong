# Qigong for Beginners: Your First Week

> **TL;DR** — Your first week of qigong is not about learning movements; it is
> about **learning to stand, breathe, and pay attention.** Practice **5–15
> minutes a day**, focusing first on slow breathing and settling the attention
> in the lower abdomen ([dantian](../glossary/dantian.md)). When you can do
> that comfortably, add a gentle movement or two. The skill — **relaxed,
> focused breathing** — is the whole foundation, and a short daily habit *may
> support* calm and body awareness more than any single long session.

Qigong (氣功, *qìgōng*) looks mysterious from the outside — slow, flowing
movements with no obvious effort. The secret is that the *visible* part is the
easy part. The real skill, and the only thing your first week needs to build, is
quieter: **breathing slowly while staying relaxed and paying attention.**

This page is a day-by-day on-ramp. If you have never practiced before, follow
it and you will finish the week with the one skill every qigong practice —
and every tai chi form — is built on.

## What "beginner" means here: entering the gate

The Chinese word for "beginner" is **入門** (*rùmén*): **入** (*rù*, "to
enter") plus **門** (*mén*, "gate, door"). Literally, it means **"entering the
gate."**

The imagery is precise and worth keeping. 門 is the familiar picture of a
door — two panels that open. 入 is a person *stepping into* something. To be a
*rùmén* beginner is not to be "below" anyone; it is to be **standing at the
gate and stepping through it.** The tradition's choice of word is generous: the
hardest part of qigong is not the technique but the *entering* — showing up,
for the first time and then repeatedly. Your first week is exactly that gate,
and walking through it is the whole point.

## The three registers

### Historical fact

Qigong as a named, modern category emerged in the mid-20th century, but the
*breath-and-movement* practices behind it — stretching, breathing, and guiding
attention — are far older, appearing in Chinese sources for well over a
thousand years under names like *daoyin* and *tuna* (see
[What Is Qigong?](what-is-qigong.md) and the [Dao Yin](dao-yin.md) entry).

### Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, the beginner's task was framed as *calming the heart and
settling the breath* (定心靜氣) before any movement could have meaning — the
same "three regulations" (body, breath, mind) that anchor all of qigong (see
[Qigong Principles](qigong-principles.md)).

### Modern explanation

The modern, grounded reading is unremarkable and useful: qigong is a
**breath-awareness and gentle-mobility practice**, and the first week simply
installs the two habits — slow breathing and body awareness — that everything
else builds on. No belief system required.

## The seven-day plan

Each day is short and builds on the previous. Adjust the minutes to your own
life — five focused minutes beats fifteen distracted ones.

| Day | Focus | What to do |
|-----|-------|------------|
| **1** | Settle and observe | Sit or stand comfortably; breathe slowly for 5 minutes; simply notice the body |
| **2** | The slow breath | Same, but lengthen the exhale gently; hands resting on the lower abdomen |
| **3** | Settle into the dantian | Keep breathing slowly; let the attention rest at the lower abdomen |
| **4** | Add release | Breathe slowly; on each exhale, consciously relax the shoulders and jaw (*song*) |
| **5** | First gentle movement | Add the "open and close the arms" movement, moving with the breath |
| **6** | A second movement | Add "lifting the sky"; keep the breath slow and the body soft |
| **7** | A short round | Do your two movements plus standing still, as one 10-minute set |

See [Qigong Breathing](qigong-breathing.md) for the breath itself and
[10-Minute Morning Qigong Routine](10-minute-morning-qigong.md) for the
movements referenced on days 5–7.

## The one skill to learn: relaxed, focused breathing

Every qigong movement in existence is built on a single foundation, and it is
not a movement at all. It is this:

> **Breathe slowly, through the nose, while letting the body stay relaxed and
> the attention rest in the lower abdomen.**

Break it down:

| Component | What it means | Why it matters |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Slow** | Around 4–6 breaths per minute, no forcing | Shifts the body toward a calm, settled state |
| **Through the nose** | Mouth gently closed | Warms and filters the air; slows the breath naturally |
| **Relaxed body** | Shoulders and jaw soft, not held | The quality called [song](../glossary/song.md) (release) |
| **Attention in the lower abdomen** | Mind resting just below the navel | The [dantian](../glossary/dantian.md), the anchor of attention |

If your first week leaves you with *only this*, the week has succeeded. The
movements are downstream of it — window dressing on a breath you already own.

## What to expect (and not to expect)

Be realistic, because unrealistic expectations end more practices than any
other cause:

- **You will probably feel calmer** — slow breathing *has been studied for*
  its relaxing, settling effect, and most people notice it quickly.
- **You will probably notice tightness** — especially in the shoulders and
  jaw — simply because you are now paying attention to them.
- **You will *not* feel anything dramatic.** There is no "energy rush" to
  chase. If a sensation is vivid or strange, it is usually just attention
  landing on a part of the body that was previously ignored.
- **You will not master movement.** That is not the goal of week one, and
  anyone promising rapid mastery is overpromising.

The honest frame: this is a *practice*, not a performance, and the benefit
accrues through repetition, not through a single experience.

## Frequently asked questions

**I'm a complete beginner — where do I actually start?**
Start with the breath. Before any movement, spend the first few days simply
standing or sitting and breathing slowly while noticing the body. This is the
foundation every qigong movement rests on, and it needs no prior skill.

**How much should I practice in my first week?**
About 5 to 15 minutes a day is ideal — short and consistent beats long and
occasional. The goal of the first week is to build the habit and learn the
breath, not to master movements.

**Do I need a teacher for the first week?**
No. The first week is about settling and breathing, which you can do on your
own with simple guidance. A teacher becomes more valuable when you move on to a
structured form like Baduanjin or tai chi.

**What if I miss a day?**
Simply resume the next day — do not double up to "catch up." Qigong is a
gentle habit, and the first week is about learning that showing up matters more
than being perfect. Consistency, not perfection, is the aim.

**What should I expect to feel?**
Most people notice they feel calmer, more aware of their breath, and more
relaxed in the body — especially the shoulders. Some feel only a mild pleasant
stretch. There is no "right" sensation to chase.

**Is qigong safe for me?**
Qigong is gentle and low-impact, and most movements can be done seated or
adapted. If you have a medical condition, recent injury, or are pregnant, it is
wise to check with a clinician before beginning any new practice.

**What happens after the first week?**
You graduate to a structured routine. The natural next step is the Baduanjin
eight-movement set or a 10-minute morning qigong routine, and eventually a tai
chi form if you want to go further.

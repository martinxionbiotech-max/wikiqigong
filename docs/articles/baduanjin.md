# Baduanjin: The Eight Pieces of Brocade

> **TL;DR** — Baduanjin (八段錦, *bāduànjǐn*), the "Eight Pieces of Brocade,"
> is the most widely practiced [qigong](../glossary/qigong.md) routine in the
> world: eight gentle movements that stretch, mobilize, and coordinate the body
> with the breath. It needs no equipment, takes 10–15 minutes, and is safe for
> nearly everyone. This page walks through each of the eight movements —
> name, movement, breath, and what to notice.

If there is one qigong routine everyone has heard of, it is Baduanjin. It is
practiced in parks, offices, and clinics across China and around the world —
eight simple movements, learned in minutes, practiced for a lifetime. This page
explains its history, then guides you through each movement one by one, and
tells you how to begin safely.

---

## What the name means

**Ba** (八) is "eight." **Duan** (段) is "section" or "piece." **Jin** (錦) is
"brocade" — fine, precious silk. So Baduanjin is "the Eight Pieces of
Brocade": eight movements as valuable to the body as woven silk is precious.

The name is poetic, not literal. The idea is that these eight movements are
the body's treasure — a complete, self-contained routine.

---

## A short history

Baduanjin has old roots but a modern form. Stretching and breathing exercises
in this family appear in Chinese sources going back roughly a thousand years,
associated with both Daoist health practice and military conditioning.

The version practiced worldwide today, however, is a **modern
standardization**. In the mid-20th century, Chinese health authorities —
building on older traditions — codified Baduanjin into a clean, teachable set
of eight movements precisely so that anyone, of any age, could practice it for
health. This is the same public-health impulse that produced the simplified
24-form tai chi.

So, as with [qigong](../glossary/qigong.md) generally: the *practice* is old,
the *standardized package* is modern.

---

## How the movements work together

Before the individual movements, the three shared principles — the "three
regulations" of [qigong](../glossary/qigong.md):

1. **Body** — soft, aligned, and relaxed; movements are slow and smooth, never
   forced or strained.
2. **Breath** — natural and coordinated; exhale on effort or stretch, inhale
   on release. Never held or forced.
3. **Mind** — attention rests on the body and breath, not on the outside world.

This is why Baduanjin, though simple, is genuine qigong: it is not eight
stretches, but eight stretches *done with breath and attention*. Each movement
below follows the same shape: a name (a poetic memory aid), a movement, a
breath cue, and "what to notice" (where your attention goes).

## The eight movements, step by step

### 1. Two Hands Hold Up the Heavens — 雙手托天理三焦

*Shuāng shǒu tuō tiān lǐ sān jiāo* — "two hands hold up the heavens to regulate
the three burners."

- **Movement:** Stand with feet together or shoulder-width, fingers interlaced,
  palms facing down. Raise the interlaced hands up the front of the body, and
  at the top turn the palms up and press gently toward the sky, rising slightly
  onto the balls of the feet. Hold for a breath, then lower the hands down the
  sides.
- **Breath:** Inhale as the hands rise; exhale slowly as they press upward;
  inhale again as they lower.
- **What to notice:** A full-body lengthening — the spine stretching from the
  tailbone through the crown, the chest opening, the shoulders releasing down
  away from the ears.

### 2. Drawing the Bow to Shoot the Eagle — 左右開弓似射鵰

*Zuǒ yòu kāi gōng sì shè diāo* — "left and right, draw the bow as if shooting
the eagle."

- **Movement:** Step into a wide, stable stance with knees soft. Cross the arms
  in front of the chest, then draw one hand into a "bow" — index finger and
  thumb extended as if aiming — while the other elbow pulls back as if drawing
  a bowstring. Look at the extended hand. Return to center and repeat to the
  other side.
- **Breath:** Exhale as you draw the bow; inhale as you return to center.
- **What to notice:** The chest and shoulders opening on the drawing side, the
  opposing shoulder relaxing, and a sense of firmness in the extended arm that
  stays soft rather than rigid.

### 3. Separating Heaven and Earth — 調理脾胃須單舉

*Tiáo lǐ pí wèi xū dān jǔ* — "to regulate the spleen and stomach, raise one
hand alone."

- **Movement:** From a shoulder-width stance, press one palm up toward the sky
  while the other palm presses down toward the earth, as if separating the two.
  Hold for a breath, then release and switch sides.
- **Breath:** Exhale as you press the two palms apart; inhale as you switch.
- **What to notice:** A long, even stretch along the side of the torso — from
  the lifted hand down through the ribs and waist to the lowered hand. The
  lower shoulder stays relaxed.

### 4. The Wise Owl Gazes Backward — 五勞七傷往後瞧

*Wǔ láo qī shāng wǎng hòu qiáo* — "to the five fatigues and seven injuries,
look backward."

- **Movement:** Stand tall, arms relaxed at your sides. Slowly turn the head
  to look behind you over one shoulder, keeping the shoulders and hips facing
  forward. Return to center and repeat to the other side.
- **Breath:** Inhale as you turn; exhale as you return to center.
- **What to notice:** A gentle rotation of the neck and upper spine — the turn
  comes from the eyes and neck, not from twisting the shoulders. Keep it slow
  and comfortable.

### 5. Sway the Head and Shake the Tail — 搖頭擺尾去心火

*Yáo tóu bǎi wěi qù xīn huǒ* — "sway the head and shake the tail to dispel
heart-fire."

- **Movement:** From a wide horse stance, place the hands on the thighs. Lean
  the torso forward slightly, then circle the trunk, leading with the head and
  letting the hips sway in the opposite direction — as if the head and "tail"
  draw a circle around a center point. Circle a few times each way.
- **Breath:** Breathe naturally and evenly throughout; do not force the
  circling to match the breath.
- **What to notice:** The spine and hips mobilizing through a full, smooth
  range. Keep the knees aligned over the feet and the movement fluid rather
  than jerky.

### 6. Two Hands Hold the Feet — 兩手攀足固腎腰

*Liǎng shǒu pān zú gù shèn yāo* — "two hands hold the feet to strengthen the
kidneys and waist."

- **Movement:** From standing, exhale and fold forward slowly, sliding the
  hands down the legs toward the feet. Let the back and hamstrings release;
  do not force the stretch. Slowly roll back up to standing.
- **Breath:** Exhale as you fold forward; inhale as you roll back up.
- **What to notice:** A gentle stretch along the entire back body — the
  hamstrings, the lower back, the spine. Keep the knees soft if they are tight,
  and come up slowly to avoid dizziness.

### 7. Clench the Fists and Glare Fiercely — 攢拳怒目增氣力

*Zǎn quán nù mù zēng qì lì* — "clench the fists and glare fiercely to increase
strength."

- **Movement:** From a stable stance, hold the fists at the waist, palms up.
  Punch one fist forward slowly, rotating the forearm so the palm ends facing
  down, while the eyes widen and focus ("glare"). Withdraw and repeat with the
  other fist.
- **Breath:** Exhale as the fist extends; inhale as it withdraws.
- **What to notice:** Gentle strength and focus — the punch is slow and
  controlled, not fast or violent. The "glare" is a wide, alert gaze, not anger.

### 8. Bouncing on the Toes — 背後七顛百病消

*Bèi hòu qī diān bǎi bìng xiāo* — "behind the back, seven bounces to eliminate
the hundred illnesses."

- **Movement:** Stand with feet together. Rise up onto the balls of the feet,
  then drop the heels to the ground with a soft, relaxed bounce. Repeat seven
  times. This is the traditional closing movement.
- **Breath:** Inhale as you rise onto the toes; exhale as the heels drop.
- **What to notice:** A gentle, settling vibration through the whole body. Keep
  the drop soft — the point is release, not impact. If balance is a concern,
  keep a chair or wall nearby.

## A safe way to begin

You can practice Baduanjin from a video or with a teacher. Here is how to
start safely:

1. **Learn the movements first, without worrying about breath.** Get the
   shapes right at whatever pace is comfortable.
2. **Add natural breathing.** Once the movements are familiar, let the breath
   coordinate loosely — out on stretch, in on release.
3. **Practice 10–15 minutes daily.** Consistency matters far more than
   intensity. Five minutes a day beats an hour once a week.
4. **Stay relaxed.** If a movement causes pain or strain, reduce the range or
   skip it. Comfort is the point, not the enemy of it.

A teacher is genuinely helpful for correcting alignment, and is recommended if
available. But the movements are simple enough that many people begin safely
with a good video.

## What the research says

Baduanjin is one of the more-studied qigong routines, though — as with all
mind–body research — the evidence varies in quality:

- Reviews of qigong (which include Baduanjin) report evidence of *possible
  benefit* for balance, psychological well-being, and some cardiovascular and
  respiratory markers (Jahnke et al., 2010).
- Studies of Baduanjin specifically *have reported* improvements in measures
  of flexibility, balance, and sleep quality in some populations, though
  results are preliminary and study quality varies.

The honest summary: Baduanjin is safe, gentle, and plausibly helpful for
relaxation, mobility, and balance — but it is a practice, not a treatment.

---

## Who should be cautious

- If you have a medical condition, especially cardiovascular, respiratory, or
  musculoskeletal, check with a healthcare professional before starting.
- If you are pregnant, consult your care provider and avoid deep forward bends
  (movement 6) and any straining.
- If you have balance problems, practice near a wall or chair, and keep
  movements small — especially movements 5 and 8.

As always: pain, dizziness, or breathlessness is a signal to stop.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does "Eight Pieces of Brocade" mean?**
Brocade is fine, precious silk — the name suggests eight movements as valuable
as woven silk for the body. It is a poetic name, not a literal description.

**How long does Baduanjin take?**
The full set of eight movements takes about 10–15 minutes at a relaxed pace. It
is short enough to practice daily.

**Do I need any equipment?**
No. Baduanjin needs only enough space to stand and move your arms. It can be
done indoors or outdoors, in ordinary clothing.

**Is Baduanjin safe for older adults?**
It is gentle and low-impact, and widely practiced by older adults. As with any
new practice, check with a healthcare professional if you have a condition, and
stop if anything hurts.

**How is it different from tai chi?**
Baduanjin is eight simple, repeated movements with no martial component and no
long sequence to memorize. Tai chi is a long, linked martial form. Baduanjin is
a common warm-up for tai chi.

**Do I breathe in a special way?**
Start with natural, relaxed breathing coordinated loosely with movement —
exhale on effort or stretch, inhale on release. Never force or hold the breath.

**How many repetitions should I do of each movement?**
A common beginner's target is four to eight repetitions per movement, done
slowly, for a total of 10–15 minutes. Consistency matters more than counting;
stop early if anything feels strained.

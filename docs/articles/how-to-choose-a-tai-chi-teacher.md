# How to Choose a Tai Chi Teacher: Lineage, Red Flags & the Right Questions

> **TL;DR** — A good tai chi teacher is less about certificates and more about
> **correctable skill**: they can *demonstrate* the movement clearly, *see and
> correct* your alignment, *explain why* (not just show how), and are *honest*
> about the limits of the art. Look for **verifiable lineage** as a starting
> point, watch how they **move**, ask about **push hands**, and walk away from
> **health claims, exaggerated lineage, or anyone who discourages questions.**
> Choosing well early saves you from unlearning bad habits later.

Tai chi is not a sport you can learn purely by watching. It is a **body skill**
learned through feedback — someone who can see the subtle misalignment you
cannot yet feel, and correct it before it becomes habit. The teacher you choose
shapes not just what you learn, but *how* you move for years to come. This page
is a practical guide: what to look for, what to ask, and what should make you
walk away.

> **Note:** this page is about *choosing* a teacher. If you are completely new
> and unsure where to begin, start with [Tai Chi for Beginners](tai-chi-beginners-first-session.md).

## What "teacher" means in tai chi

In Chinese, the word usually rendered "teacher" is **師父** (*shīfu*, also
spelled *shifu*). It joins **師** (*shī*, "teacher, master, model") with **父**
(*fù*, "father"). Literally, it means **"teacher-father."**

The imagery is worth understanding, because it shapes how the tradition treats
teaching. 師 pictures a person who is a *model* — someone whose own body, manner,
and character demonstrate the thing being taught, not just someone who talks
about it. The 父 adds a note of **durable, personal responsibility**: the
teacher-mentor who takes a long-term interest in the student's development, the
way a parent's responsibility does not end when a lesson does.

This is not about worship. It is a statement of a *relationship*, not a
*status*. In practice, it means tai chi teaching was historically an
apprenticeship — close, personal, and long — rather than a classroom where one
person lectures and everyone copies. When you choose a teacher, you are
choosing the quality of that close feedback, which is exactly why the choice
matters so much.

## Why the teacher matters more in tai chi than in many activities

Most exercise can be learned from a screen because the *goal* is visibly
obvious — lift the weight, hold the pose, run the distance. Tai chi is
different. The goal is **internal quality** (relaxation, alignment, the smooth
transfer of weight), which is nearly invisible from the outside.

This has a specific consequence:

| What you can learn alone | What you need a teacher for |
|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| The rough sequence of a form | Correcting alignment you can't feel |
| The names of postures | Releasing hidden tension (*song*) |
| Basic breathing | Whether your weight transfer is truly smooth |
| The general idea | The "why" behind each movement |
| Getting started | Avoiding bad habits before they set |

The first column is why a good video can help. The second is why a teacher is
difficult to replace — **you cannot correct what you cannot perceive, and a
beginner cannot perceive most of what needs correcting.**

## The three registers

### Historical fact

Tai chi developed in Chen Village, Henan, from the 17th century, carried
forward through named lineages — Chen, Yang, Wu (吳), Wu (武), and Sun — that
today make up the [five recognised styles](tai-chi-five-styles.md). Teaching was
traditionally **personal and apprenticed**, transmitted within families and to
chosen disciples, which is why lineage became the way skill was tracked and
trusted (see the full account in [What Is Tai Chi?](what-is-tai-chi.md)).

### Traditional interpretation

In the traditional frame, the *shīfu* relationship is about more than technique —
it is a model of character and conduct, in which the student learns not only
movement but patience, humility, and calm. This is best read as a *teaching
philosophy* rather than a separate technique.

### Modern explanation

The modern, practical reading is plain: tai chi is a **movement skill best
learned through skilled feedback**, and a good teacher is simply a reliable
source of that feedback. No one needs to wear a uniform or use honorifics to
benefit from a teacher who can see, correct, and explain.

## What to look for in a tai chi teacher

### 1. They can demonstrate — and explain — clearly

A teacher should be able not just to *perform* the movement, but to **break it
down** and say *why* the weight moves this way, why the knee stays over the
foot, why the shoulders stay down. A teacher who can only say "copy me" is
offering demonstration without instruction. Look for the ability to take a
movement apart and put it back together for a beginner.

### 2. They correct, not just count

In a good class, the teacher **walks and adjusts**: moving your shoulder,
nudging your hip, showing you the difference between where you *are* and where
the posture *is*. If a class is only about memorizing a sequence with no
individual correction, you are learning choreography, not tai chi.

### 3. They look settled and relaxed when they move

Watch the teacher move. Do they look **rooted** (settled, weighted, grounded)
or stiff and forced? Do they look **relaxed** — the quality of
[song](song.md) — or tight and showy? The body does not lie: a teacher who
moves with softness and stability *is* showing you what they understand (see
[Rooting (Connection to the Ground)](rooting.md) and [Posture & Alignment](tai-chi-posture-alignment.md)).

### 4. They welcome questions

Tai chi is full of "why." A teacher who is comfortable saying "I am not sure —
let me think about that" is more trustworthy than one with a ready, mystical
answer for everything. The honest teacher is secure enough to admit the edges
of their knowledge.

### 5. They are honest about the limits of the art

This connects directly to the site's editorial standard. A teacher worth your
time will say that tai chi *may support* health and *has been studied for*
balance and relaxation — and will **not** promise to cure, treat, or heal
anything. Overclaiming is the single clearest sign of a teacher to avoid.

## Red flags to walk away from

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|----------|----------------|
| **Health or healing claims** ("cures arthritis," "reverses illness") | Overclaims the art; violates basic honesty about its limits |
| **Exaggerated or unverifiable lineage** ("grandmaster of a lineage no one can trace") | Lineage inflation is common; modest verifiable lineage is worth more |
| **Discourages questions** ("don't ask, just copy") | Shuts down the learning that makes tai chi worth learning |
| **No correction, only counting** | You are learning choreography, not skill |
| **Pressure to buy extras** (expensive uniforms, special courses, "energy" treatments) | A teaching relationship turning into a sales funnel |
| **Contempt for other styles or teachers** | Signs of insecurity, not authority |

One red flag is not always fatal — a skilled teacher might be brusque, for
example. But a *pattern* of several is a clear signal to keep looking.

## Questions to ask before you commit

**About lineage and training**

- "What tradition or lineage do you teach in, and who was your main teacher?"
- "How long have you trained, and how long have you been teaching?"

**About teaching itself**

- "How do you structure a class — is there individual correction?"
- "Do you teach push hands?" ([Push Hands](push-hands.md) is the traditional
  way skill is *tested*; a teacher who teaches it is confident in their art,
  not just their performance.)
- "What should I expect to be able to do after six months?"

**About your own fit**

- "Can I watch or try a class before committing?"
- "What is your approach to students with my age/fitness level?"

The way a teacher *answers* these questions matters as much as the answers.
Evasiveness, defensiveness, or mystification are themselves information.

---

## A checklist for your first visit

- [ ] Does the teacher correct individual form, or just lead from the front?
- [ ] Can they explain *why*, in plain words, not just demonstrate?
- [ ] Do they look balanced, settled, and relaxed — not stiff or showy?
- [ ] Do they answer questions openly, including admitting uncertainty?
- [ ] Do they talk honestly about health (hedged, not "cures")?
- [ ] Does the class leave you feeling calm and capable, not confused or sore?
- [ ] Would *you* feel comfortable asking this person to slow down and explain?

If most boxes are ticked, you have found a teacher worth learning from.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a tai chi teacher, or can I learn from videos?**
You can start with videos, but a teacher is far better for the early stages. A
teacher can see and correct alignment mistakes you cannot feel yourself, and
can answer the questions that arise live. Videos work best as a supplement once
the fundamentals are set.

**What qualifications should a tai chi teacher have?**
There is no single universal tai chi certification, and a certificate is not a
guarantee of skill. More useful than paper credentials are verifiable lineage,
teaching experience, and whether the teacher can demonstrate the movement
clearly and correct your form.

**How important is lineage?**
Lineage matters because it tells you what tradition the teacher is working in
and where their training comes from. But lineage is a starting point, not a
destination — a clear, patient teacher with a modest lineage may serve a
beginner better than a famous one with a distant lineage claim.

**What are the red flags to avoid in a tai chi teacher?**
Common red flags include medical or healing claims, exaggerated lineage,
dismissal of questions, a class that is only about memorizing moves with no
correction, and any pressure to buy expensive extras. A good teacher is honest
about the limits of the art.

**Should a tai chi teacher be Chinese or have trained in China?**
Not necessarily. Skill in tai chi comes from training and teaching ability, not
nationality. Many excellent teachers have no Chinese background, just as some
poor teachers do. Judge the teaching and the practice, not the ethnicity.

**How do I know if a teacher actually has skill?**
Watch them move — do they look settled, balanced, and relaxed rather than stiff
or showy? Ask about push hands, which is the traditional way skill is tested.
Above all, notice whether they can explain why a movement is done a certain
way, not just demonstrate it.

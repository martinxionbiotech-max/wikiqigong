# Qigong vs Meditation

> **TL;DR** — Qigong and meditation train the **same core skill — steadying
> attention** — through different means. Meditation does it through
> **stillness**; qigong does it through **movement and breath** (though qigong
> has still forms too). Meditation has the larger, stronger evidence base;
> qigong suits people who find sitting still difficult.

Qigong is often called "moving meditation," and the phrase captures a real
truth — but it also blurs a real distinction. This page explains how the two
relate, where they overlap, and how to choose.

---

## Key facts at a glance

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Shared skill | Both train steadying attention — the "place, drift, return" cycle |
| Meditation | Training the mind, using stillness |
| Qigong | Training mind *and* body together, using movement and breath |
| Stronger evidence base | Meditation (for attention and stress) |
| Suits restless people | Qigong (adds movement to attention) |
| Can you do both? | Yes — they are companions, not competitors |

---

## The shared core

Both practices train the same fundamental skill: **the ability to place
attention and return it when it wanders.**

In meditation, you typically sit and return attention to the breath. In qigong,
you move and return attention to the movement, the breath, and the body. The
"place, drift, return" cycle is identical — only the anchor differs.

---

## Side-by-side comparison

| Dimension | Qigong (氣功) | Meditation (靜坐) |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Primary anchor** | Movement and breath | Breath, body, or a chosen object |
| **Posture** | Standing, seated, or moving | Usually seated (or walking) |
| **Physical component** | Yes — movement | Minimal (except walking forms) |
| **Body benefit** | Balance, mobility, circulation | Mostly indirect |
| **Attention training** | Through movement | Through stillness |
| **Evidence base** | Growing, preliminary | Large and strong |

---

## Where they overlap — and where they split

**The overlap is the attention training.** Both teach you to notice a
wandering mind and gently return it — the single most valuable skill either
practice offers. Both also use the [breath](../glossary/breathing.md) as the
primary anchor, and both traditionally aim to "settle the
[shen](../glossary/shen.md)" — to calm the mind.

**The split is the body.** Qigong engages the body directly — moving,
stretching, standing, balancing — which gives it physical benefits (balance,
mobility, circulation) that seated meditation does not target. Meditation
engages the mind more exclusively, which is why its evidence base for
attention and stress is larger and stronger.

A useful way to hold it:

- **Meditation** = training the mind, using stillness.
- **Qigong** = training the mind *and body together*, using movement and
  breath.
- **Still qigong** (like [zhan zhuang](../articles/zhan-zhuang.md)) = the
  bridge between the two.

---

## Which should you choose?

**Choose meditation if…**
- Your primary goal is calming the mind and training attention.
- You want the practice with the largest, strongest evidence base.
- You prefer stillness and have a quiet place to sit.

**Choose qigong if…**
- You find sitting still uncomfortable or restless.
- You want physical benefits — balance, mobility, breath — alongside mental
  calm.
- You prefer an active practice.

**Choose both if…** you can. They are not competitors but companions: qigong
settles the body so the mind can sit; meditation deepens the attention qigong
cultivates.

---

## What the research says

- **Meditation** has a large and mature evidence base, *studied for* its
  effects on attention, stress, and anxiety — with effect sizes that are real
  but modest.
- **Qigong** shows evidence of *possible benefit* for balance, psychological
  well-being, and some physiological markers, with study quality that varies
  (Jahnke et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2013).

The honest summary: meditation is the more thoroughly validated practice for
mental calm; qigong adds a physical dimension with a growing evidence base.
Neither is a treatment for any condition.

---

## The bottom line

| If you want… | Choose |
|--------------|--------|
| Strongest evidence for calming the mind | **Meditation** |
| Physical + mental benefits together | **Qigong** |
| Something active (can't sit still) | **Qigong** |
| Deepest attention training | **Meditation** |
| Both | **Both** |

They are two expressions of one skill: **learning to be present.**

---

## Who should be cautious

Both are generally safe, but a note for honesty:

- Some people find intense breath focus or long sitting uncomfortable; if so,
  shorten sessions and go gently.
- Meditation and qigong are supportive practices, not substitutes for
  professional mental-health care.
- If a practice causes distress, dizziness, or discomfort, stop and reconsider.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is qigong just moving meditation?**
Partly. Qigong applies meditative attention to movement and breath, which is
why it is sometimes called moving meditation. But qigong also has still forms
(like zhan zhuang) that are essentially standing meditation.

**Which is better for calming the mind?**
Both train the same core skill of steadying attention. Seated meditation is
the most direct, studied method for attention training; qigong adds movement,
which suits people who find sitting still difficult.

**Can I do both?**
Yes, and many practitioners do — seated meditation and qigong complement each
other perfectly. Qigong settles the body so the mind can sit; meditation
deepens the attention qigong cultivates.

**Do I need to sit in lotus to meditate?**
No. A chair is perfectly fine. The Chinese tradition (jìngzuò, "quiet
sitting") is flexible about posture — comfort and a straight, relaxed spine
matter more than leg position.

**Is qigong a form of meditation?**
In the broad sense, yes — it trains attention in the present moment. But it is
a distinct practice family with its own movement and breath methods, not a
subset of seated meditation.

**Which is better for anxiety?**
Both have been studied for stress and anxiety reduction, with meditation
generally having the larger and stronger evidence base. Neither is a treatment
— they are practices that may support well-being.

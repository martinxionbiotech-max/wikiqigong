# The Taiji Diagram (太極圖): The Yin-Yang Symbol Explained

> **TL;DR** — The taiji diagram (太極圖, *tàijí tú*) is the swirl symbol most
> people call the "yin-yang." It pictures two paired, opposite qualities — **yin
> and yang** — that **contain a seed of each other** (the two dots) and **flow
> continuously into one another** (the curl). It is a diagram of **dynamic
> balance and mutual relation**, not a static "good versus evil" emblem, and its
> name — *taiji*, "supreme ultimate" — is the same term that names the art of
> tai chi.

The taiji diagram is one of the most recognized images in the world — and one
of the most quietly misunderstood. It is almost always read as a simple
"opposites" emblem: light versus dark, good versus bad. But the symbol is more
precise, and more interesting, than that.

This page explains what the diagram actually shows, where it comes from, how to
read its parts, and what it has to do with tai chi. For the underlying concept
of the two forces, see [Yin and Yang](yin-yang.md).

## What the diagram shows

The taiji diagram is a circle divided into two interlocking, curling shapes —
conventionally one **black (yin)** and one **white (yang)** — with a small dot
of the opposite color inside each.

Read at its core, the diagram makes **three claims**:

1. **Two paired qualities** — the world of change is made of paired, opposite
   but complementary qualities (yin and yang).
2. **Each contains the other** — the dot inside each half shows that nothing is
   purely one thing; every yin holds a seed of yang, and every yang holds a
   seed of yin.
3. **They turn into each other** — the curling, tapering shapes suggest
   continuous rotation: yang rises, peaks, and becomes yin; yin deepens, turns,
   and becomes yang.

Together, these three claims describe **dynamic balance** — not a static
freeze-frame of two separate things, but a picture of two things in motion,
each already becoming the other. That is the diagram's central idea, and the
part most easily lost when it is flattened into a mere "opposites" logo.

## The three registers

### Historical fact

The *idea* of yin and yang is very old, traceable to early Chinese texts over
two thousand years old, where the terms began as descriptions of the shady and
sunny sides of a hill before expanding into a general framework of paired
qualities. The specific **taiji diagram** we recognize today — the black-white
swirl with two dots — has a more recent and debated history.

It is most often associated with the Song-dynasty philosopher **Zhou Dunyi**
(周敦頤, 1017–1073) and his short work *Taijitu Shuo* (太極圖說, "Explanation of
the Taiji Diagram"), which used a diagram of nested circles to describe how the
undivided whole (*wuji*) gives rise to *taiji*, and *taiji* to yin and yang.
However, the standard "curling fish" swirl popular today is a later,
simplified rendering — its exact origins are uncertain, and scholars note that
the diagram's visual forms have changed across centuries.

### Traditional interpretation

In classical teaching, the diagram is a picture of the cosmos in miniature.
*Taiji* — "supreme ultimate" — is the balanced whole that holds yin and yang in
motion. From that whole, the two forces arise, interact, and cycle; the diagram
is sometimes surrounded by the **eight trigrams** (八卦, see
[Bagua](../glossary/bagua.md))
to show how those two forces combine into the patterns of change. The two dots
express the doctrine that **each extreme already contains its opposite**, so
that nothing can remain purely yin or purely yang for long.

### Modern explanation

Read without metaphysics, the diagram is a strikingly effective **visual model
of complementary opposites in dynamic equilibrium.** It expresses ideas that
modern systems thinking echoes: opposites that are interdependent rather than
independent, systems that self-regulate by cycling between two poles (activity
and rest, effort and recovery, exertion and restoration). The diagram's
enduring value is that it *shows* these relationships in a single glance —
which is why it still reads clearly to people with no knowledge of Chinese
philosophy.

## How to read each part

The symbol breaks down cleanly:

| Part | What it shows |
|------|---------------|
| The circle (outer boundary) | The whole — the bounded totality that contains everything |
| The black half (yin) | Dark, rest, contraction, cold, receptivity, the inner |
| The white half (yang) | Bright, activity, expansion, warmth, the outward |
| The dot in each half | Each side already contains a seed of the other |
| The S-shaped divide | A curved, not straight, boundary — movement, not division |
| The tapering curl | Each side grows, peaks, and turns into the other |

Notice the **S-shaped divide.** It is curved, not a straight line, and this is
deliberate. A straight line would split the circle into two fixed, static
regions — a picture of separation. The curve, by contrast, suggests **flow**:
two currents circling each other, each swelling and narrowing as it goes. It
turns a "division" into a "circulation." This is the difference between reading
the symbol as "two opposite halves" and reading it as "one whole in motion."

---

## The two dots: the seed of the other

The small dot in each half is the symbol's most important detail and the one
most often missed. It states plainly: **within yin, there is yang; within yang,
there is yin.**

This carries two everyday meanings:

1. **Nothing is pure.** A "rest" day is not absence of activity but a different
   kind of it; a calm mind still has thought in it; a warm season still holds
   cool days. The dot refuses the idea of clean, absolute categories.
2. **Each extreme is the seed of its own turning.** Yang at its peak begins to
   tip into yin, and vice versa. The dot is the *germ* of that change already
   present inside the thing.

This is why the diagram is a picture of **process, not product** — of things
becoming, not things being.

## Why it shares its name with tai chi

The term **太極** (*tàijí*) means "supreme ultimate" — the balanced whole from
which the two forces arise. It is the *exact same two characters* that name the
martial and health art of **tai chi** (太極拳, *tàijíquán*, "supreme ultimate
fist").

This is not a coincidence. Tai chi is, at a deep level, a **training in the
balance the diagram pictures**: the continuous interplay of empty and full,
weight-bearing and weight-free, extending and withdrawing, moving and
stillness — always keeping the two in flowing equilibrium rather than lurching
between extremes. The art is the diagram *performed with the body.* See
[What Is Tai Chi?](what-is-tai-chi.md) for the art's own account, and
[Wuji (The Uncarved State)](wuji.md) for the still, undivided state that
precedes the two forces.

## What the diagram is *not*

Because the symbol travels widely, it picks up misreadings worth correcting:

- **It is not "good versus evil."** Yin and yang are complementary, not moral.
  Neither side is the "bad" one; each is necessary.
- **It is not a static "two opposites" logo.** The dots and the curve insist on
  movement and mutual containment — the opposite of a frozen division.
- **It is not a claim that "everything is balanced at all times."** It pictures
  a tendency toward turning, not a guarantee of equal proportions in any given
  moment.
- **It is not uniquely "mystical."** Its core idea — opposing qualities that
  interpenetrate and cycle — is a familiar, testable observation about the
  world.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does the taiji diagram mean?**
It pictures how two paired, opposite qualities — yin and yang — contain each
other and continuously flow into one another. It is a diagram of dynamic
balance and mutual relation, not a static "good vs evil" emblem.

**Is the taiji diagram the same as the yin-yang symbol?**
Yes — the taiji diagram (太極圖, tàijí tú) is the proper name for the swirl
symbol most people call the "yin-yang symbol." The swirl is one common modern
rendering of a much older idea.

**Who invented the taiji diagram?**
No single person invented the idea; it grew over centuries. The classic
"curling fish" swirl still recognized today is often linked to Song-dynasty
philosopher Zhou Dunyi and his Explanation of the Taiji Diagram, though the
exact origins of the standard image are debated.

**What do the two dots in the symbol mean?**
The small dot in each half shows that nothing is purely one thing: within the
black (yin) there is a seed of white (yang), and within the white there is a
seed of black. Each side contains the beginning of the other.

**Does the taiji diagram represent good and evil?**
No. Yin and yang are not good and evil. They are complementary, interdependent
qualities — dark and light, rest and movement, cold and warm — neither of which
is "better." The diagram shows their balance, not a moral contest.

**Why does the symbol look like two fish?**
The swirl is often read as two "curling fish" because each half narrows to a
point that curls into the other, suggesting continuous rotation. It is a visual
way of showing that yin and yang flow and turn into each other, like water or
breath.

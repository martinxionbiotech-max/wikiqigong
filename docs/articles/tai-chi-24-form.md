# Tai Chi 24-Form: A First Guide

> **TL;DR** — The Tai Chi 24-form (二十四式太極拳, *èrshísì shì tàijíquán*)
> is a **simplified, standardized Yang-style tai chi sequence of 24
> movements**, created in 1956 to make tai chi easier to learn and teach. One
> round takes about **four to six minutes** at a slow, even pace, and most
> beginners can memorize the sequence in a few months. It is the most widely
> practiced tai chi form in the world — the routine most people learn first.

If you have ever seen a group in a park moving slowly and smoothly through a
sequence of postures, you were almost certainly watching a version of the
24-form. It is the international standard entry point to tai chi — short
enough to memorize, complete enough to teach the real method.

---

## What the name means

**Èrshísì** (二十四) is "twenty-four." **Shì** (式) means "form," "posture,"
or "pattern." **Tàijíquán** (太極拳) is tai chi itself — the "supreme
ultimate fist." So the name is simply "the twenty-four-form tai chi": a
counted sequence of 24 postures, each flowing into the next without pause.

The form is also commonly called the **simplified form** or the **Beijing
form**, reflecting its 1956 origin as a standardized teaching sequence.

---

## The three registers

Like every core practice on this site, the 24-form is clearest in three
separated layers.

### Historical fact

The 24-form was created in **1956** by a committee of tai chi experts
convened by China's national sports authorities. Their brief was to produce a
**short, standardized sequence** suitable for mass teaching. The committee
based the form on the **Yang-style long form**, the most widely practiced
family lineage, selecting and simplifying its movements to remove repetitions
and difficult techniques.

The result — 24 movements that can be performed in under six minutes — spread
rapidly, becoming the standard introductory form in China and, over the
following decades, throughout the world. It is now the most taught tai chi
sequence globally.

### Traditional interpretation

The 24-form is not ancient — it is a 20th-century distillation. But the
*method* it teaches is the traditional one: the sequence is a vehicle for the
same internal principles the classical lineages transmit. Each posture
expresses [song](../glossary/song.md) (relaxed release), [rooting](../glossary/gen.md)
(sinking into the ground), and the continuous alternation of [yin and
yang](../articles/yin-yang.md) (empty and full, open and close). The form is
understood as a moving meditation in which the body and breath and attention
are trained as one.

### Modern explanation

Read in modern terms, the 24-form is a **slow, low-impact, whole-body movement
sequence** that has been *studied for* its effects on balance, mobility, and
relaxation — the same body of research that supports tai chi generally. Its
slow, continuous weight-shifting trains balance and leg strength; its
coordinated breathing and attention are a form of gentle stress reduction.
Because it is standardized, it is also unusually well suited to research and
to consistent, repeatable practice.

## The structure of the form

The 24 movements are grouped into a beginning, a middle of signature
postures, and a closing. This is a **simplified overview** for orientation —
the exact choreography is best learned from a teacher or video:

| Group | Movements (simplified) | What they train |
|-------|------------------------|-----------------|
| **Opening** | Commencing, Part the Wild Horse's Mane (×3), White Crane Spreads Wings | Rooting, arm–leg coordination, balance |
| **Middle (grasping & stepping)** | Brush Knee, Play the Lute, Step Back and Repulse Monkey, Grasp the Sparrow's Tail, Single Whip, Wave Hands Like Clouds | Weight shifting, waist turning, continuity |
| **Middle (kicking & balance)** | Kick with Heel, Heel Kick, Snake Creeps Down, Golden Rooster Stands on One Leg | Leg strength, single-leg balance |
| **Closing** | Cross Hands, Closing | Centering, settling, finishing |

**The key point:** the sequence matters less than the *quality* of each
posture. The form is a container for the principles — move slowly, stay soft,
sink your weight, and keep the breath easy.

## A simple way to begin

You can start your first session without memorizing anything:

1. **Stand comfortably**, feet shoulder-width apart, knees soft, shoulders
   relaxed — the same opening stance as [Zhan Zhuang](../articles/zhan-zhuang.md).
2. **Lift and lower the arms** slowly with the breath a few times, as in the
   opening movement, to settle in.
3. **Shift your weight** from one foot to the other a few times, keeping the
   movement slow and the spine upright, to feel the rooting.
4. **Learn one or two postures at a time** from a teacher or a good video —
   beginning with "Commencing" and "Part the Wild Horse's Mane."
5. **Chain what you know** into a slow, continuous loop, adding one posture at
   a time as each becomes comfortable.

The full form grows from these small pieces. Speed and flow come later; first
comes correctness and relaxation.

## How it relates to other practices

| Practice | Relationship to the 24-form |
|----------|------------------------------|
| **Yang long form** | The source — the 24-form is a simplified, shortened version |
| **Tai Chi Five Styles** | The 24-form belongs to the Yang family branch |
| **Zhan Zhuang** | The still standing practice that builds the root the form needs |
| **Qigong Breathing** | The breath technique that runs through the movements |

The 24-form sits at the meeting point of tai chi's martial heritage and its
modern health practice — structured enough to teach, gentle enough for nearly
anyone.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"It is a complete martial system."** It is a standardized health and
   introductory form. The traditional long forms carry the deeper martial
   curriculum.
2. **"It is ancient."** It dates to 1956. The *principles* are ancient; the
   sequence is a modern teaching tool.
3. **"Faster is more advanced."** In tai chi the practice is to move slowly
   and continuously — speed is not the point.
4. **"You must memorize it perfectly before you benefit."** The benefits come
   from regular, relaxed practice of whatever you know — not from perfect
   recall of all 24 postures.

---

## Who should be cautious

The 24-form is gentle and low-impact, but a responsible note:

- **Keep the knees soft and aligned with the toes** — never let a knee collapse
  inward during weight shifts.
- If you have a **balance, joint, or cardiovascular condition**, check with a
  healthcare professional before starting.
- **Lower the depth** of any stance to match your current strength; you can
  practice most of the form seated if standing is difficult.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the Tai Chi 24-form in one sentence?**
The Tai Chi 24-form (二十四式太極拳) is a simplified, standardized Yang-style
tai chi sequence of 24 movements, created in 1956 to make tai chi easier to
learn and teach, and now the most widely practiced tai chi form in the world.

**Why was it created?**
It was commissioned by China's sports authorities in 1956 to provide a short,
standardized introductory form. The traditional Yang long form can exceed 100
movements and take years to learn; the 24-form distills the essentials into a
routine most beginners can memorize in a few months.

**Which style is it based on?**
The Yang style, the most popular family lineage of tai chi, known for its
smooth, even-paced, large-frame movements. Several movements were simplified
or omitted from the traditional Yang long form.

**How long does it take to learn?**
Most beginners can learn the sequence in a few months of regular practice —
much faster than the traditional long forms. Learning the *sequence* and
*refining* it are different things; mastery develops over years.

**How long does one round take?**
Performed at a slow, even pace, one round of the 24-form takes about four to
six minutes. Many practitioners do one to three rounds per session.

**Do I need a teacher?**
A qualified teacher is the best way to learn correct alignment and avoid bad
habits, but the form is gentle enough that beginners can also start with a
good video or class and practice slowly. If you have health concerns, check
with a professional first.

**How is it different from the Yang long form?**
The 24-form keeps the essential movements of the Yang style but removes
repetitions and difficult techniques, making it shorter, more accessible, and
easier to standardize for teaching.

# Liu Zi Jue: The Six Healing Sounds

> **TL;DR** — Liu Zi Jue (六字訣, *liù zì jué*), the "Six Healing Sounds,"
> is a traditional [qigong](../glossary/qigong.md) that pairs **six spoken
> sounds** — *xu, he, hu, si, chui, xi* — with slow, gentle movement. Each
> sound is traditionally linked to a different organ system, but the core
> mechanism is simpler than that sounds: **a long, shaped exhale** that slows
> the breath and settles the attention. It needs no equipment, takes 10–15
> minutes, and is gentle enough for nearly everyone.

Most qigong you will meet is about *movement*. Liu Zi Jue is about the
**breath** — and specifically about the sound the breath makes. The idea is
strikingly simple: make six quiet, drawn-out sounds, each with a different
mouth shape, while moving gently. This page explains the history, teaches the
six sounds, and shows how to practice safely.

---

## What the name means

**Liu** (六) is "six." **Zi** (字) is "character" or "written word." **Jue**
(訣) means "secret," "formula," or "knack" — the kind of instruction passed
from teacher to student. So Liu Zi Jue is literally "the six-character
secret": six syllables, each treated as a precise breathing formula.

The name itself is honest about its origin — a *transmitted method*, taught
personally rather than read from a diagram. That is why the "formula" is in
the sound, not in a posture.

---

## The three registers

Like every core practice on this site, Liu Zi Jue is clearest in three
separated layers.

### Historical fact

The six-sound method is first clearly described by the Daoist scholar **Tao
Hongjing** (陶弘景, 456–536 CE) in his *Yangxing Yanming Lu* (養性延命錄,
"Record of Nourishing Nature and Prolonging Life"), where breathing methods
using voiced sounds are recorded as part of the therapeutic repertoire of the
period. The method was later taken up and refined by the great Tang-dynasty
physician **Sun Simiao** (孫思邈, 581–682 CE), who associated specific
breathing and sound methods with health practice in his writings.

The system as taught today — six named sounds, each with a standard organ
association and a standardized movement sequence — was consolidated and
standardized in the **20th century**, and a modern version was published by
China's sport and health authorities in the early 2000s as one of the
recognized health qigong routines.

### Traditional interpretation

In traditional teaching, each of the six sounds is associated with one of the
organ systems of Chinese medicine, arranged through the **Wu Xing** (五行,
"five phases") framework:

| Sound | Character | Pinyin | Traditional association |
|-------|-----------|--------|-------------------------|
| *Xu* | 嘘 | xū | Liver (肝, gān) |
| *He* | 呵 | hē | Heart (心, xīn) |
| *Hu* | 呼 | hū | Spleen (脾, pí) |
| *Si* | 呬 | sī | Lungs (肺, fèi) |
| *Chui* | 吹 | chuī | Kidneys (腎, shèn) |
| *Xi* | 嘻 | xī | Triple burner (三焦, sān jiāo) |

Classically, the sounds were understood to "drain" or "clear" excess from
their associated organ system, restoring the smooth flow of [qi](../glossary/qi.md)
— the same logic of harmonizing balance found throughout Chinese health
thought. These are **functional systems named after organs**, not anatomical
claims about literal organs.

### Modern explanation

Read in modern terms, Liu Zi Jue is a **breathing-pattern practice**. Each
sound is simply a way to lengthen and shape the exhale — the mouth shape and
voicing naturally slow the outflow of air. Long, controlled exhalation is
itself *studied for* its effects on relaxation and the nervous system. The
organ associations are best understood as a traditional mnemonic and
organizing framework, not a claim about the physical organs; the practice
works as gentle breath and attention training whether or not you adopt the
traditional map.

## The six sounds, one by one

The heart of the method. Each sound is made **quietly**, on a long, slow
exhale, with a specific mouth shape:

| Sound | Mouth shape | Rough English sound | What to notice |
|-------|-------------|---------------------|----------------|
| *Xu* (嘘) | Lips relaxed, corners slightly drawn | like a soft "shoo" | A gentle, diffuse release |
| *He* (呵) | Mouth open, jaw dropped | like "huh" (breathy) | Warmth and openness in the exhale |
| *Hu* (呼) | Lips rounded, pushed forward | like "who" | A full, rounded stream of air |
| *Si* (呬) | Teeth lightly together, lips wide | like "sss" | A thin, focused stream |
| *Chui* (吹) | Lips tightly rounded, small opening | like "chway" | A narrow, forceful but soft stream |
| *Xi* (嘻) | Mouth relaxed, slight smile | like "hee" (breathy) | An even, gentle release |

**The key point:** the sound is not loud. It is a whisper — enough to shape
the breath, not enough to strain the throat. Inhale naturally through the
nose; exhale slowly through the mouth on the sound.

## A simple practice sequence

You can practice the six sounds in about 10–15 minutes. Here is a safe,
beginner-friendly version:

1. **Stand comfortably**, feet shoulder-width apart, knees soft, shoulders
   relaxed. Or sit upright if standing is difficult.
2. **Inhale gently through the nose**, letting the belly relax.
3. **Exhale slowly through the mouth**, making one sound — *xu* — for the
   full length of the exhale. Let the sound trail off naturally.
4. **Repeat** the sound two to six times, then move to the next.
5. **Work through all six sounds** in order: xu, he, hu, si, chui, xi.
6. **Finish** with a few slow, natural breaths and a moment of quiet standing.

Add gentle arm movement only if you like — the traditional routines pair each
sound with a soft arm raise or press. For beginners, the breath and sound are
the whole practice.

## How it relates to other practices

Liu Zi Jue sits naturally in the same family as the rest of the site:

| Practice | Emphasis | Relationship to Liu Zi Jue |
|----------|----------|----------------------------|
| **Liu Zi Jue** | Breath + sound | The breath-centered method |
| **Baduanjin** | Movement + stretch | Often practiced as a companion warm-up |
| **Zhan Zhuang** | Stillness + posture | The still root; Liu Zi Jue adds voiced breath |
| **Dao Yin** | Movement + breath | The older ancestor both descend from |

All of them rest on the same three regulations — body, breath, and attention.
Liu Zi Jue simply makes the **breath** the lead instrument.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"The sounds cure specific organs."** They do not. The organ associations
   are a traditional organizing framework. The practice is breath and
   relaxation training, not treatment.
2. **"You must say the sounds loudly."** The opposite — they are whispered,
   long exhales. Loud, forced sounds strain the throat.
3. **"The pronunciation must be exact to work."** The sounds shape the
   exhale; precision matters less than a slow, comfortable breath.
4. **"It is a religion."** It has Daoist historical roots, but the modern
   practice requires no belief system — only breath and attention.

---

## Who should be cautious

Liu Zi Jue is gentle, but a responsible note:

- **Breathing is comfortable, never forced.** If you feel dizzy or
  light-headed, you are over-breathing — shorten the exhale and rest.
- If you have a **respiratory or heart condition**, check with a healthcare
  professional before starting any breathing practice.
- You can do the whole practice **seated** if standing or balance is a
  concern.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Liu Zi Jue in one sentence?**
Liu Zi Jue (六字訣), the "Six Healing Sounds," is a traditional Chinese
breathing qigong that pairs six spoken sounds with slow, gentle movement, each
sound traditionally associated with a different organ system.

**What are the six sounds?**
Xu (嘘, xū), He (呵, hē), Hu (呼, hū), Si (呬, sī), Chui (吹, chuī), and Xi
(嘻, xī). In traditional teaching each is linked to a different organ system:
liver, heart, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and the triple burner respectively.

**Do I need to believe in qi to practice it?**
No. The slow breathing and gentle movement work as relaxation and breath
training regardless of framework. The sounds are simply a way to shape the
exhale.

**How long does a session take?**
A full set of six sounds, with a few repetitions each, takes about 10–15
minutes. It is short enough to practice daily.

**Is Liu Zi Jue safe?**
It is gentle and low-impact, with no equipment. As with any breathing
practice, keep the breath comfortable and never strain or hyperventilate.
Check with a healthcare professional if you have a respiratory or heart
condition.

**How is it different from Baduanjin?**
Baduanjin emphasizes movement and stretching; Liu Zi Jue emphasizes the breath
and the spoken sound, with movement kept small and supportive. They are often
practiced together.

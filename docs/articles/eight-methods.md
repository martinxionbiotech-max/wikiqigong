# The Eight Methods & Five Steps (Thirteen Postures)

> **TL;DR** — Tai chi's movements are not random. They are built on a classical
> skeleton of **eight methods** (*bāfǎ*, 八法) and **five steps** (*wǔbù*,
> 五步). Together they are the **Thirteen Postures** (*shísānshì*, 十三勢) — the
> technical vocabulary behind every form. The eight methods are *peng, lu, ji,
> an, cai, lie, zhou, kao*; the five steps are *advance, retreat, look left,
> look right, central equilibrium*. You do not need to memorize them to begin —
> but they are the map that turns "slow waving" into a precise system.

Most introductions to tai chi describe it as slow, graceful movement. Few
explain *what the movements are actually doing*. The answer is the eight
methods and five steps — the classical framework that gives every posture its
name and intention. This page explains that framework in plain English, with
the Chinese terms, what each one means, and why it still matters for a modern
health practitioner.

## The eight methods (八法)

The eight methods describe **directions and qualities of force**. The first
four are the "square" cardinal directions; the last four are the "corners."
Each has a Chinese name, a pinyin reading, and a one-line English gloss.

| # | Pinyin | Chinese | English | Direction | What it does |
|---|--------|---------|---------|-----------|--------------|
| 1 | *peng* | 掤 | Ward-off | Forward | Expands outward to receive and deflect |
| 2 | *lu* | 捋 | Roll back | Backward | Yields and draws the incoming force past |
| 3 | *ji* | 擠 | Press | Forward | Presses with combined hands, shoulder to hands |
| 4 | *an* | 按 | Push | Down-forward | Pushes downward and forward with both palms |
| 5 | *cai* | 採 | Pluck | Corner-down | Pulls sharply downward, like picking fruit |
| 6 | *lie* | 挒 | Split | Corner | Splits force in two opposing directions |
| 7 | *zhou* | 肘 | Elbow | Corner | Uses the elbow to strike or redirect |
| 8 | *kao* | 靠 | Shoulder (bump) | Corner | Leans with shoulder, back, or hip |

### The four cardinal methods

- **Peng (掤, ward-off)** — an *expanding* quality: the arm and body open
  outward to receive and deflect force without collapsing or bracing. It is
  often called the "mother" of the eight, the base the others modify.
- **Lu (捋, roll back)** — a *yielding* quality: you absorb and draw the
  incoming force past you, redirecting it rather than meeting it head-on.
- **Ji (擠, press)** — a *pressing* quality: force is delivered by pressing one
  hand (or the body) toward the other, shoulder and hip aligned behind it.
- **An (按, push)** — a *pushing* quality: both palms press downward and
  forward from a rooted stance.

### The four corner methods

- **Cai (採, pluck)** — a sharp *downward pull*, like plucking fruit from a
  tree; it drops the opponent's structure suddenly.
- **Lie (挒, split)** — a *splitting* of force in two directions at once, like
  wringing or snapping.
- **Zhou (肘, elbow)** — the use of the **elbow** to strike or redirect at
  close range.
- **Kao (靠, shoulder)** — a **bump or lean** with the shoulder, upper back, or
  hip, used when very close.

## The five steps (五步)

The five steps describe **how the body and direction move**. They map the
compass: four directions plus the center.

| # | Pinyin | Chinese | English | What it means |
|---|--------|---------|---------|---------------|
| 1 | *jìn bù* | 進步 | Advance | Step forward |
| 2 | *tuì bù* | 退步 | Retreat | Step backward |
| 3 | *zuǒ gù* | 左顧 | Look left | Shift/step to the left |
| 4 | *yòu pàn* | 右盼 | Look right | Shift/step to the right |
| 5 | *zhōng dìng* | 中定 | Central equilibrium | Stay rooted and centered |

**Central equilibrium** (中定) is the anchor of the other four — the quality of
remaining balanced and rooted no matter which direction you move. In many
readings, it is not "another step" but the condition that makes all movement
possible, closely tied to [rooting](../glossary/gen.md) and
[song](../glossary/song.md).

## Where the "thirteen" comes from

Eight methods plus five steps equals **thirteen** — the number of the
**Thirteen Postures** (十三勢, *shísānshì*). This is the classical name for
tai chi's complete technical system, and it appears in the traditional tai chi
writings (the "tai chi classics") as the art's organizing scheme.

The number is also read symbolically. In traditional Chinese cosmology, the
eight methods correspond to the **eight trigrams** (八卦, *bāguà*) and the five
steps to the **five elements/phases** (五行, *wǔxíng*) — the same yin–yang
framework that underlies the whole art (see [yin-yang](../glossary/yin-yang.md)
and [wu-xing](../glossary/wu-xing.md)). Whether one takes this as deep
structure or as a mnemonic, it shows how tightly tai chi's movements are tied
to its philosophy.

## The three registers

### Historical fact

The thirteen-posture scheme is part of the classical tai chi vocabulary,
recorded in the traditional writings attributed to the art's early lineages
(see [what is tai chi](what-is-tai-chi.md) and
[tai chi five styles](tai-chi-five-styles.md)). As with tai chi's origins, the
exact dating of these texts is uncertain; the framework itself is genuinely
old and shared across the major styles.

### Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, the eight methods and five steps are understood as expressions
of yin and yang: each method is a particular balance of soft and firm, empty
and full, and the five steps are the directions through which that balance
moves. Practitioners learn to feel, not just perform, each quality — the
difference between "doing a ward-off shape" and "being ward-off."

### Modern explanation

Read without the traditional language, the framework is a **movement taxonomy**:
a consistent naming system for the directions and qualities of force in the
form. That is genuinely useful — it turns a long, complex sequence into a
finite set of recognizable elements, which is how teachers correct movement
and how practitioners develop precision.

## Why it matters for a health practitioner

You do not need the eight methods to *begin* tai chi, but they help at every
later stage:

- **They name what you are feeling.** "This movement is a ward-off" is more
  useful than "this movement is arm-wave number seven."
- **They sharpen intention.** Knowing a movement is *peng* (expanding) changes
  how you do it — with openness rather than force.
- **They reveal the structure.** The methods and steps are why tai chi is a
  *system*, not a collection of poses; they connect directly to
  [tai chi principles](tai-chi-principles.md).
- **They train balance.** The five steps, anchored by central equilibrium, are
  exactly the weight-shifting and stability work behind tai chi's
  well-documented balance benefits (see
  [tai chi for older adults](tai-chi-for-older-adults.md)).

In short: the eight methods and five steps are the difference between waving
your arms slowly and *doing tai chi*.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the eight methods of tai chi?**
They are eight named techniques: peng (ward-off), lu (roll back), ji (press),
an (push), cai (pluck), lie (split), zhou (elbow), and kao (shoulder/bump).
Four are the "square" cardinal directions; four are the "corners" or diagonal
directions.

**What are the five steps of tai chi?**
They are five ways of moving the body and direction: jin bu (advance), tui bu
(retreat), zuo gu (look left), you pan (look right), and zhong ding (central
equilibrium). Together they map the directions of movement.

**What are the Thirteen Postures?**
The eight methods plus the five steps — eight plus five equals thirteen, the
number of the "thirteen postures" (十三勢). It is the classical name for tai
chi's complete technical framework.

**Do I need to memorize these to do tai chi?**
No. Beginners learn them through the form without naming them. Knowing the
names helps later — it is a map of what the movements are doing, not a
prerequisite.

**Is the eight-methods framework about fighting?**
It originated as a martial map, but today it is most useful as a way to
understand the structure and intention of each movement — which also makes the
health practice clearer and more precise.

**Which method is most important?**
Traditionally, peng (ward-off) is considered the foundation that the others
modify. But the framework is a set, and its value is in how the eight interact,
not in ranking them.

# Tai Chi Five Styles: Chen, Yang, Wu, Wu/Hao, and Sun

> **TL;DR** — There are five major family styles of tai chi: **Chen** (oldest,
> most martial), **Yang** (most widely practiced, gentle and even), **Wu 吳**
> (compact, forward-leaning), **Wu 武/Hao** (small and scholarly), and **Sun**
> (newest, upright and lively). All five descend from the Chen family art and
> share one set of principles — relaxation, rooting, and slow whole-body
> movement. Beginners are almost always pointed to **Yang style** first.

When someone says they "do tai chi," the follow-up question is usually: *which
style?* It is a fair question, because [tai chi](../glossary/tai-chi.md) is not
one thing — it is a family of five major styles that share a philosophy but
differ in pace, posture, and history. This page sorts them out: where they came
from, what makes each distinct, and which one to learn first.

---

## The five styles at a glance

| Style | Chinese | Founder / era | Character | Best known for |
|-------|---------|---------------|-----------|----------------|
| **Chen** | 陳式 | Chen Wangting, 17th c. | Oldest; alternates slow/fast, soft/hard | Spiral "silk reeling" force, low stances |
| **Yang** | 楊式 | Yang Luchan, 19th c. | Even, slow, gentle, upright | World's most practiced style; the 24-form |
| **Wu (吳)** | 吳式 | Wu Quanyou, 19th c. | Small, compact, slight forward lean | Precision in a small frame |
| **Wu (武)/Hao** | 武式 | Wu Yuxiang, 19th c. | Small, subtle, scholarly | Refined internal detail |
| **Sun** | 孫式 | Sun Lutang, early 20th c. | Upright, lively, agile footwork | Newest style; blends internal arts |

A note on the names: the two "Wu" styles are written with *different Chinese
characters* and come from *different families*. They are not variants of one
style — they are two unrelated lineages that happen to share a romanized name.

---

## A short, honest history

All five styles trace back to one place: **Chenjiagou** (陳家溝), "Chen Family
Village," in Henan province. The picture is a family tree, not five separate
inventions.

- **Chen style** is the root. The earliest well-documented tai chi is
  associated with the Chen family, and **Chen Wangting**, a retired military
  officer of the 17th century, is traditionally credited with synthesizing
  earlier martial and breathing practices into what became Chen-style tai chi.
  (The older story of the Daoist immortal Zhang Sanfeng is founding myth, not
  documented history — see [What Is Tai Chi?](what-is-tai-chi.md).)
- **Yang Luchan** learned in Chen Village in the 19th century and adapted the
  art into the softer, even-paced form most people picture today. This **Yang
  style** is the trunk from which most later styles branched.
- **Wu Quanyou** and his son **Wu Jianquan** developed **Wu (吳) style** from
  Yang roots, favoring smaller, more compact movements.
- **Wu Yuxiang** studied with the Yang and Chen lineages and, with **Hao
  Weizhen**, refined a small, subtle **Wu (武)/Hao style** prized for its
  internal precision.
- **Sun Lutang**, already a master of two other internal arts, created **Sun
  style** in the early 20th century — the newest of the five, notable for its
  upright, agile, "follow-step" footwork.

The version most people learn today is more recent still: in **1956**, Chinese
sports authorities created the **24-form** ("simplified tai chi"), condensing
Yang-style movements into a short sequence anyone could learn. This is why
"tai chi in the park" looks so uniform around the world — most of it is
standardized Yang style.

## What distinguishes each style

### Chen (陳式) — the root

Chen style is the oldest and the most visibly martial. Its hallmark is the
alternation of slow and fast, soft and hard movements, and a signature quality
called **silk reeling** (*chán sī*, 纏絲) — continuous, spiraling force through
the limbs, like drawing silk from a cocoon. Stances are often lower and wider,
and there is an audible emphasis on explosive release in some forms. It is the
most physically demanding of the five.

### Yang (楊式) — the most practiced

Yang style smoothed Chen's rhythm into an even, slow, gentle flow, with an
upright posture and a high, comfortable stance. It is the style most people
mean when they say "tai chi," and it is the basis of the standardized 24-form.
Its accessibility is exactly why it spread worldwide.

### Wu (吳式) — small and compact

Wu (吳) style keeps movements small and economical, with a slight forward lean
in the upper body and a higher stance. It suits people who prefer a narrower,
less physically demanding frame, and it is often taught for precision and
internal focus.

### Wu (武)/Hao — small and scholarly

Wu (武)/Hao style is the most refined and least common of the five, with tiny,
subtle movements and a strong emphasis on internal detail over outward
flourish. It has historically been practiced by a smaller circle of dedicated
students.

### Sun (孫式) — upright and lively

Sun style is the newest, and it shows the influence of the other internal arts
its founder mastered (xingyi and bagua). Its signature is upright posture,
lively, agile footwork, and a distinctive "follow step" where the back foot
steps up to meet the front. It is often recommended for people who like a more
mobile, less earthbound feel.

## Which style should you learn first?

For most beginners, the answer is **Yang style** — or its simplified 24-form
derivative. Here is the reasoning in one table:

| If you want… | Consider |
|--------------|----------|
| The most common, easiest-to-find teaching | **Yang** (24-form) |
| A gentle, even, upright practice | **Yang** |
| A more explicitly martial, physically demanding art | **Chen** |
| A small, compact frame with less wide stepping | **Wu (吳)** |
| Deep internal refinement, smaller circle of teachers | **Wu (武)/Hao** |
| An upright, lively, mobile feel | **Sun** |

The honest advice: **the best style is the one you can learn well from a good
teacher and practice consistently.** The differences between styles are real,
but they are far smaller than the difference between practicing regularly and
not practicing at all.

## What all five share

Despite their differences, the five styles are branches of one tree. They all
rest on the same principles, explored in depth in [Tai Chi
Principles](tai-chi-principles.md):

1. **Relaxation** (*song*, 鬆) — releasing unnecessary tension while keeping
   structure (see [Song](song.md)).
2. **Rooting** — weight settling down through the feet, stable and grounded.
3. **Whole-body movement** — motion originating from the center and flowing to
   the limbs.
4. **Continuity** — unbroken, smooth flow, "like drawing silk."
5. **Yin–yang shifting** — the mindful exchange of weight between substantial
   and insubstantial.

Every style is an expression of these; none of them requires belief in
[qi](../glossary/qi.md) to practice. They are observable, physical skills.

## Who should be cautious

All five styles are low-impact and adaptable, but a note for honesty:

- **Chen style** is the most physically demanding — its low stances and
  explosive releases may not suit everyone, especially knees.
- If you have joint or balance issues, choose a gentler style (Yang or Sun)
  and keep stances high.
- As with any movement practice, check with a healthcare professional if you
  have a medical condition, and stop if anything hurts.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many tai chi styles are there?**
Five major family styles are universally recognized: Chen, Yang, Wu (吳), Wu
(武)/Hao, and Sun. All five descend from the Chen family art and share the same
underlying principles.

**Which tai chi style is best for beginners?**
Yang style — and its simplified 24-form derivative — is the most common
recommendation for beginners, because it is even-paced, upright, and gentle.
Chen style is more physically demanding and more explicitly martial.

**What is the oldest tai chi style?**
Chen style, from Chenjiagou village in Henan province. The earliest
well-documented tai chi is associated with the Chen family, and every other
major style descends from it.

**What is the difference between the two Wu styles?**
They are different families with different surnames. Wu (吳) style descends
from Wu Quanyou and is known for compact movements and a slight forward lean.
Wu (武)/Hao style descends from Wu Yuxiang and is small, subtle, and scholarly.

**Is one style more "authentic" than the others?**
No. Chen is the oldest, but all five are legitimate lineages with continuous
documented transmission. "Authenticity" is about a teacher's lineage and
training, not about which family name is on the form.

**Do I need to learn a specific style to get tai chi's health benefits?**
No. The health and balance benefits of tai chi have been studied across
styles, and the core principles — relaxation, rooting, slow movement — are
shared by all five. Choose the style you can learn well and practice
consistently.

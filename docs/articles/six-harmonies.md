# The Six Harmonies (六合): How Body, Intention, and Qi Unify

> **TL;DR** — The Six Harmonies (六合, *liù hé*) are six pairings that make a
> movement **one thing instead of many parts.** Three are **internal** — mind
> (*xin*) with intention (*yi*), intention with qi, and qi with force (*li*).
> Three are **external** — **hands with feet, elbows with knees, shoulders with
> hips.** Together they describe **whole-body coordination**: the inside
> (attention and breath) and the outside (the skeleton) moving as a single,
> connected unit.

Every Tai Chi or Qigong teacher, at some point, says some version of the same
thing: *don't move the hand by itself — move it with the foot.* *The elbow
follows the knee.* *Where the intention goes, the body follows.* Behind these
scattered images stands a single idea with a name: the **Six Harmonies**
(六合, *liù hé*), "the six unifications."

This page explains what the six pairings are, where the idea comes from, and
why it matters day to day. For the three-layer model it builds on, see
[Xing, Yi, Qi (Form, Intention, Energy)](../glossary/xing-yi-qi.md), and for
the art that most explicitly trains it, see
[What Is Tai Chi?](what-is-tai-chi.md).

## The three registers

### Historical fact

The *term* "six harmonies" (六合, *liù hé*) is an old one in Chinese thought —
it can mean the six directions (the four cardinal points plus up and down) in
early cosmology. As a *body-method* idea, however, the six harmonies are most
strongly associated with **Xingyiquan** (形意拳, "Form-Intention Boxing"), one
of the three great internal martial arts alongside Tai Chi and Baguazhang.

Traditional accounts trace the pairings' prominence in Xingyi tradition to
several influential teachers of the Qing dynasty and after, though — as with
much internal-arts lore — the exact origins are not firmly documented and the
same principle circulates widely across Tai Chi, Bagua, and Qigong, stated in
slightly different words by different schools. The *idea* — that the body
should move as a coordinated whole — is common to all of them, whatever name it
is given.

### Traditional interpretation

Classically, the six harmonies are read as the recipe for *whole-body force*:
power is not produced by any single limb but by the unification of the whole
person. The three **internal** harmonies describe the chain that runs from mind
to body:

- **心與意合** (*xīn yǔ yì hé*) — the mind (*xin*) unites with intention (*yi*);
- **意與氣合** (*yì yǔ qì hé*) — intention unites with qi;
- **氣與力合** (*qì yǔ lì hé*) — qi unites with force (*li*).

And the three **external** harmonies describe the skeleton aligning so that the
whole structure can carry that force:

- **手與足合** (*shǒu yǔ zú hé*) — hands unite with feet;
- **肘與膝合** (*zhǒu yǔ xī hé*) — elbows unite with knees;
- **肩與胯合** (*jiān yǔ kuà hé*) — shoulders unite with hips.

In traditional teaching the message is that neither set works alone: the
external alignments are the *container*, and the internal chain is what *fills*
it. Only together do they produce what the internal arts call "whole-body
power" (整勁, *zhěng jìn*).

### Modern explanation

Read without metaphysics, the six harmonies describe an ordinary and observable
principle of skilled movement: **proximal-and-distal coordination.** When a
throwing athlete or a skilled dancer moves, the force travels from the ground
up through the legs and trunk and out to the hands — the whole body is the
engine, not the arm alone.

The external harmonies are a checklist for that: hand with foot (top with
bottom), elbow with knee (middle with middle), shoulder with hip (root with
root). The internal harmonies are a description of *attention*: mind directing
intention, intention guiding the breath and felt quality, and that quality
finally expressing as relaxed, connected force. In modern terms, qi here is the
felt smoothness and connection of an attentive, well-aligned movement — no
belief in a literal energy substance is required to train it.

## The three internal harmonies

The internal set is the part people find most abstract — and it is really a
description of how **attention becomes action**, in three links:

| Pairing | Chinese | What it describes |
|---------|---------|-------------------|
| Xin – Yi (心–意) | mind – intention | Attention becomes a directed aim |
| Yi – Qi (意–氣) | intention – qi | The aim guides the breath and felt quality |
| Qi – Li (氣–力) | qi – force | That quality expresses as coordinated power |

The chain matters because of its *direction*: it moves from the inside out.
First you place the mind, then you set an intention, then the movement and
breath organize behind it, and only then does force appear — as a result of the
chain, not the start of it. This is the opposite of "trying harder": force is
the *last* link, produced by the earlier three, not the thing you grab first.

See [Xing, Yi, Qi](../glossary/xing-yi-qi.md) for the closely related
form–intention–qi progression, and [Yi (Intention)](../glossary/yi.md) for the
middle link on its own.

## The three external harmonies

The external set is concrete and physical — three joint pairings that keep the
upper and lower body moving as one:

| Pairing | Chinese | What to feel |
|---------|---------|--------------|
| Hands – Feet (手–足) | hands with feet | The hand and the foot arrive and turn together |
| Elbows – Knees (肘–膝) | elbows with knees | The elbow and knee bend, rise, and settle together |
| Shoulders – Hips (肩–胯) | shoulders with hips | The shoulder and hip rotate as one unit |

The easiest way to *feel* the external harmonies is to notice when they break.
Lift a hand while the same-side hip stays frozen and the force comes entirely
from the shoulder — that is a "broken" hand, disconnected from the ground. Now
let the turn begin at the foot, travel up the leg, through the [kua (hips)](../glossary/kua.md),
and arrive at the hand — the hand and foot now move "together," and the push
feels effortless. That is the difference the six harmonies describe.

The shoulders–hips pairing is usually taught first, because it is the largest
and easiest to feel; the elbows–knees and hands–feet pairings refine the same
idea down the limb. See [Posture & Alignment](tai-chi-posture-alignment.md) for
how this connects to the vertical axis.

## Why the pairing between "internal" and "external" is the point

The two sets are not two separate lists; they are the *inside* and *outside* of
the same act. The external harmonies give the body a structure; the internal
harmonies give that structure a *direction and a felt quality.* Traditional
teaching often summarizes this in a single image: **"the outside moves, the
inside leads it"** — *wài dòng nèi yǐn*.

In plain terms: alignment alone is empty (a perfectly aligned body can still be
moving lifelessly), and intention alone is airy (a strong intention with no
structure collapses). The six harmonies are the reminder to train both, always
together. This is the heart of [Neigong (internal work)](neigong.md), and of
the [Song (relaxed release)](song.md) that lets the pairings actually unify
instead of fighting each other.

## How it shows up in practice

The six harmonies are not a thing you "do" separately from your normal
practice — they are a quality you notice *inside* it. Here is how they surface
in three common activities:

- **Standing practice ([Zhan Zhuang](zhan-zhuang.md))** — you hold a still
  posture and audit the pairings: are the hands floating (disconnected from the
  feet), or is the whole line from sole to fingertip connected?
- **The slow solo form** — each turn should start low (foot, hip) and arrive
  high (hand) together, so that no limb moves ahead of the rest.
- **Two-person [Push Hands](push-hands.md)** — you feel, in contact, whether
  your partner moves as one piece or as a disconnected arm; the whole-body
  mover is much harder to uproot.

A useful mental test: **move one hand across your body and watch the opposite
foot.** If the foot is not involved in the shift of weight that the hand needs,
the hand is "alone." Reversing that — letting the foot and hip drive the turn
that carries the hand — is the six harmonies in one small movement.

## Common misconceptions

- **"It's six separate rules to memorize."** They are six *relationships*, and
  they blend into one felt quality of whole-body movement. No one practices by
  reciting all six on every move.
- **"The internal harmonies are mystical energy talk."** Read plainly, they
  describe attention becoming directed movement — an ordinary, observable
  process, not a supernatural one.
- **"It only matters for martial arts, not health practice."** Whole-body
  coordination — moving from the ground up rather than from the shoulder — is
  precisely what makes Tai Chi and Qigong gentle on the joints and supportive
  of balance and ease.
- **"It comes only from Xingyiquan."** The name and the most explicit
  formulation come from the Xingyi tradition, but the principle is shared
  across the internal arts and Qigong.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the Six Harmonies in Tai Chi and Qigong?**
The Six Harmonies (六合, liù hé) are six pairings that link the internal and
external parts of a movement. Three are "internal" — mind (xin) with intention
(yi), intention with qi, and qi with force (li). Three are "external" — the
hands with the feet, the elbows with the knees, and the shoulders with the
hips.

**What are the three internal harmonies?**
The three internal harmonies pair mind with intention, intention with qi, and
qi with force. They describe how attention becomes directed effort, how
intention moves the breath and felt quality, and how that quality finally
expresses as coordinated physical power.

**What are the three external harmonies?**
The three external harmonies pair hands with feet, elbows with knees, and
shoulders with hips. They describe structural alignment: the joints above and
below should move as one coordinated unit rather than as isolated limbs.

**Why are they called "harmonies" rather than "rules"?**
Because they are not rigid positions but live relationships. The point is not
to freeze the body into a fixed shape but to have the paired elements move
together, arrive together, and turn together — like voices in harmony rather
than a single fixed note.

**Do I need to memorize all six to practice?**
No. Most teachers introduce them one at a time and often via images — "the
elbow follows the knee," "the hand and foot arrive together." Over time they
become one felt quality of whole-body coordination rather than a six-item
checklist.

**Is the Six Harmonies idea from Tai Chi or from another art?**
It is most strongly associated with the internal martial arts, especially
Xingyiquan (Form-Intention Boxing), but the same principle is used across Tai
Chi, Baguazhang, and Qigong to describe whole-body integration.

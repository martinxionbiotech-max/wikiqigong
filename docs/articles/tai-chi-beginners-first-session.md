# Tai Chi for Beginners: A First Session

> **TL;DR** — You can start tai chi today, safely, in about **20 minutes**.
> The first session is not about memorizing a form; it is about learning to
> stand, breathe, and move with [song](../glossary/song.md) (relaxed release)
> and [rooting](../glossary/gen.md). Warm up gently, stand still for a few
> minutes, move slowly through a few basic postures, and finish calm.

Most people put off starting tai chi because they think they need a teacher, a
class, or weeks of preparation. The truth is gentler: your **first session**
can happen at home, right now, with nothing but comfortable clothes and a few
square meters of floor. This page walks you through a complete, safe first
session — what to do, what to notice, and what to skip.

---

## What tai chi is, in one breath

Tai chi (太極拳, *tàijíquán*) is a Chinese mind–body practice usually
described as a slow, continuous sequence of movements. Underneath the
movement is a *method*: relaxed, aligned posture; slow, natural breathing; and
calm, gathered attention.

You do not need to learn the long, traditional form to begin. The first
session is about **feeling** the method, not performing it. For the fuller
picture of the art itself, read [What Is Tai Chi?](what-is-tai-chi.md).

---

## Before you begin: the honest checklist

| Item | Why it matters |
|------|----------------|
| Loose, comfortable clothes | Tight clothing restricts breath and movement |
| Flat, thin-soled shoes (or bare feet) | You want to feel the floor through the soles |
| A clear space, ~2 × 2 meters | Enough room to step without tripping |
| A quiet room, phone on silent | Attention is the whole practice |
| No pain, dizziness, or illness | If in doubt, check with a professional first |

Nothing here is optional-but-fancy. It is all practical: tai chi is done with
attention, and attention needs a little physical and mental room.

## Your first session, minute by minute

Here is the full 20-minute session. Go slowly. The times are guides, not
rules.

### Minutes 0–3: Settle and stand

Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, parallel, knees soft (not locked). Let
your arms hang at your sides. Take a few slow breaths and just *arrive*.

This standing posture is the seed of everything. It is the same stance as
[zhan zhuang](../articles/zhan-zhuang.md) (站樁, "standing like a post") —
the standing-meditation practice that builds the foundation.

### Minutes 3–6: Gentle warm-up

Move slowly through each, repeating two or three times:

1. **Roll the shoulders** — up, back, and down in slow circles.
2. **Turn the head** — gently left and right, chin level.
3. **Sway the arms** — let them swing loosely, like hanging ropes.
4. **Shift the weight** — rock softly from one foot to the other.

The goal is to loosen the joints and find [song](../glossary/song.md) —
relaxed release without collapse.

### Minutes 6–10: Stand and find your root

Return to standing still. This is the heart of the session:

1. Soften the knees and let the weight settle down into the soles of the feet.
2. Drop the shoulders, soften the chest, and let the breath sink into the
   lower abdomen — the [dantian](../glossary/dantian.md).
3. Feel the feet grow heavier and more connected to the floor.

You are now practicing [rooting](../articles/rooting.md) (根, *gēn*): the
grounded, stable connection that makes tai chi work. Stay here a few minutes,
returning your attention to the feet whenever it drifts.

### Minutes 10–16: A few slow movements

Stand with feet shoulder-width, knees soft. Move at **half speed**, breathing
naturally. Do each movement three to five times:

**1. Raise and lower the arms ("opening").** Let both hands float up in
front of you to chest height, palms down, then sink them gently back down.
Inhale as they rise; exhale as they lower.

**2. Shift the weight side to side.** Step your feet a little wider. Slowly
shift your weight to the right foot, then the left, letting the body sway
like a reed. Keep the knees soft and the breath even.

**3. "Wave hands" step.** Step one foot slightly forward and shift forward
and back, letting the arms swing gently like seaweed in water. Then step the
other foot.

Do not worry about doing these "correctly." Worry about moving *slowly,
softly, and without holding your breath*.

### Minutes 16–20: Close and rest

Come back to a comfortable standing posture (or sit). Place one hand over the
lower abdomen. Take five slow, easy breaths. Let the body settle. Notice how
you feel — calmer, softer, more grounded than when you started.

## The three registers: where this practice comes from

Tai chi's first session is simple, but it rests on deep roots. Here is the
honest background, in three clearly separated layers.

### Historical fact

Tai chi as a named art is usually traced to the Chen family village in Henan
province, with surviving lineages formalized from roughly the 17th to 19th
centuries (the exact origins are debated and not firmly documented). The
modern, widely taught simplified forms — including the 24-form — were
developed in the 20th century to make the art accessible to general health
practice.

### Traditional interpretation

Traditional teaching frames tai chi as the cultivation and circulation of
[qi](../glossary/qi.md) (氣), coordinated with the balance of
[yin and yang](../glossary/yin-yang.md). The slow movements are described as
a moving meditation in which body, breath, and mind unify — the outer form
expressing an inner state of calm, soft, connected strength.

### Modern explanation

Read without metaphysics, tai chi is a low-impact form of slow, coordinated
movement plus attention. Modern research links this combination to better
balance, mobility, and relaxation (see references below). The first session
— standing, breathing, and slow movement — is simply the most direct way to
begin training those qualities.

## What to notice (and what to ignore)

Your only job in the first session is to *feel*. Here is what to pay
attention to, and what to let go of.

| Notice this | Ignore this |
|-------------|-------------|
| The soles of your feet | Whether your form is "perfect" |
| The weight sinking downward | Whether you look graceful |
| The breath staying slow and even | Whether you are "doing it right" |
| Releasing the shoulders and jaw | Whether you memorized the moves |
| A growing sense of calm | Whether you are "making progress" |

The beginner's mistake is to treat tai chi as choreography to master. Treat it
as a feeling to find, and the choreography will come later, almost on its own.

---

## Common mistakes in the first session (and how to fix them)

Everyone makes these. Here they are, named, so you can catch yourself and
correct course.

| Mistake | What it feels like | The fix |
|---------|--------------------|---------|
| **Holding the breath** | You realize you are tense in the chest | Exhale first, then let the inhale come on its own |
| **Locking the knees** | The legs feel rigid, the body top-heavy | Soften the knees a little, sink the weight down |
| **Rushing the movement** | The arms swing like you are in a hurry | Cut the speed in half, then half again |
| **Forcing a "correct" shape** | Shoulders up, jaw tight, trying hard | Drop the shoulders, soften the jaw, re-find song |
| **Drifting into thought** | You are planning your day mid-movement | Return attention to the soles of the feet |
| **Overdoing the breathing** | Dizziness or light-headedness | Return to natural breathing; never force it |

None of these means you are "bad at it." Catching one and correcting it *is*
the practice — that moment of noticing and returning is exactly what tai chi
trains.

## Where to go next after this session

Your first session is a doorway, not a destination. A sensible path from here:

1. **Repeat this 20-minute session** for a week or two — consistency beats
   ambition at this stage.
2. **Lengthen the standing** — extend the rooting section from a few minutes
   to five or ten. It is the part with the most carry-over.
3. **Learn a short form** — when the basics feel familiar, find the
   simplified 24-form (a teacher, a class, or a careful video) and begin
   adding movements one at a time.
4. **Pair it with qigong** — a short [qigong](../articles/qigong-principles.md)
   routine is an ideal warm-up and complements the tai chi method.

## Why start with standing?

A first session built on standing and slow movement is not a shortcut — it is
the traditional starting point, and it is the part with the strongest
evidence.

- **Standing still** trains [rooting](../articles/rooting.md) and body
  awareness — the foundation of balance.
- **Slow movement** trains smooth, relaxed coordination, the quality tai chi
  is famous for.
- **Slow breathing** activates the body's relaxation response (see
  [Qigong Breathing](../articles/qigong-breathing.md)).

Balance training is one of the best-studied benefits of tai chi, and it begins
right here, in the first session.

---

## Who should be cautious

Tai chi is broadly safe, but the same sense applies as with any movement:

- If you have balance problems, practice near a wall or chair, or do the
  movements seated.
- If you have knee, hip, foot, or back issues, keep the stance high and
  comfortable, and keep the range of motion small.
- If you have heart or respiratory conditions, check with a healthcare
  professional first, especially around the breathing.
- Stop anything that causes sharp pain, dizziness, or breathlessness.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should I do in my very first tai chi session?**
Keep it simple: a gentle warm-up, a short standing practice to find relaxation
and balance, a few slow basic movements, and a calm finish. Twenty minutes is
plenty — you are learning to feel the body, not to memorize a form.

**Do I need special clothing or equipment?**
No. Wear loose, comfortable clothes and flat, thin-soled shoes (or go barefoot
on a safe surface). You need enough floor space to take a step in each
direction, and nothing else.

**How do I breathe during tai chi?**
Breathe naturally and slowly through the nose, into the lower abdomen, and let
the breath follow the movement — inhale as you open or rise, exhale as you
close or sink. Never force or hold the breath.

**How long should my first session be?**
Around 15–20 minutes is ideal. It is better to do a short, relaxed session
daily than a long, tense one occasionally. Stop if you feel pain, dizziness,
or strain.

**Is tai chi safe for older adults or beginners with no fitness?**
Yes. Tai chi is low-impact, slow, and adaptable to almost any fitness level —
which is part of why it is so widely studied in older adults. Check with a
healthcare professional if you have balance, joint, or heart conditions.

**What is the most important thing to get right as a beginner?**
Relaxation (song, 鬆). Everything else — balance, smooth movement, calm
breathing — follows from releasing unnecessary tension. Do not chase perfect
choreography; chase a soft, open, grounded feeling.

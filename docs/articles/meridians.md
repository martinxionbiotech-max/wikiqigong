# Meridians: The Body's Channels

> **TL;DR** — Meridians (經絡, *jīngluò*) are the network of channels through
> which Chinese medicine describes [qi](../glossary/qi.md) — the body's
> functional energy — as flowing. They are best understood as a **functional
> map** of how the body's systems connect, not a physical structure. You do
> not need to believe in them to practice
> [qigong](../glossary/qigong.md) or [tai chi](../glossary/tai-chi.md).

Alongside qi, "meridians" is the word most people know from Chinese medicine —
and the one most often misunderstood. You will hear it in acupuncture, in
qigong, and in tai chi. This page explains what meridians are, where the idea
came from, and how to read it honestly today.

## What the name means

Meridians are called 經絡 (*jīngluò*) in Chinese — a two-part word worth
unpacking, because it tells you what the system is for.

- **Jing** (經) originally means the *warp* — the long, load-bearing threads
  that run lengthwise through a loom. By extension it means a "main channel,"
  "passage," or "classic text."
- **Luo** (絡) means a "net" or "network" — the fine threads that connect and
  bind.

So 經絡 is literally **"channels and network"**: a set of main channels (the
jing) connected by a finer web of branches (the luo). The English word
"meridian" is a later Western translation — it entered English usage in the
early 20th century through French writers who borrowed the word for a
geographical line of longitude.

---

## The three registers

Like every core concept on this site, meridians are clearest in three
separated layers.

### Historical fact

The channel concept developed gradually in Chinese medicine. The foundational
text is the *Huangdi Neijing* (黃帝內經, "Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic"),
compiled around the Han dynasty (roughly the 2nd century BCE to the 1st
century CE) but reflecting earlier material. It describes a system of
channels through which qi and blood move, with points along them where the
flow could be influenced — the ancestor of modern acupuncture.

Over centuries the map was elaborated and standardized. By the late classical
period, Chinese medicine described **twelve primary channels** (十二經脈,
*shí'èr jīngmài*), each associated with an organ system, plus **eight
"extraordinary vessels"** (奇經八脈, *qí jīng bā mài*) — a second layer that
includes the two best-known midline channels, the *du mai* (督脈, "Governing
Vessel") along the back and the *ren mai* (任脈, "Conception Vessel") along
the front.

### Traditional interpretation

Classically, health is described as the smooth, balanced flow of qi through
the channels, and illness as blockage, deficiency, or misdirection of that
flow. The twelve primary channels are the map of that flow, each named for an
organ system:

| Meridian | Chinese | Pinyin | Yin/Yang | Associated system |
|----------|---------|--------|----------|-------------------|
| Lung | 肺經 | fèi jīng | Yin (hand) | Lungs |
| Large Intestine | 大腸經 | dàcháng jīng | Yang (hand) | Large intestine |
| Stomach | 胃經 | wèi jīng | Yang (foot) | Stomach |
| Spleen | 脾經 | pí jīng | Yin (foot) | Spleen |
| Heart | 心經 | xīn jīng | Yin (hand) | Heart |
| Small Intestine | 小腸經 | xiǎocháng jīng | Yang (hand) | Small intestine |
| Bladder | 膀胱經 | pángguāng jīng | Yang (foot) | Bladder |
| Kidney | 腎經 | shèn jīng | Yin (foot) | Kidneys |
| Pericardium | 心包經 | xīnbāo jīng | Yin (hand) | Pericardium |
| Triple Burner | 三焦經 | sānjiāo jīng | Yang (hand) | Triple burner |
| Gallbladder | 膽經 | dǎn jīng | Yang (foot) | Gallbladder |
| Liver | 肝經 | gān jīng | Yin (foot) | Liver |

These are **functional systems named after organs**, not anatomical claims
about the literal organs. The "triple burner" (三焦), for example, has no
single physical counterpart — it names a functional division of the torso.

### Modern explanation

No anatomical structure matching the meridian map has ever been found under a
microscope. The most honest modern reading is that meridians are a **clinical
and teaching map** — a way of organizing the body's relationships into a
coherent, teachable system.

The modern research picture is genuine but modest: some studies report that
acupoints and channel lines correlate in places with **connective-tissue
planes, fascial layers, and nerve pathways**, and acupuncture itself *has been
studied for* pain and other symptoms with mixed, preliminary results. But the
"meridian" as a literal physical channel is **not established**. It is best
treated as a model: useful for organizing practice and treatment, not a
substance you could dissect.

## Meridians in qigong and tai chi

You do not need to adopt the meridian map to practice, but it explains several
things you will hear:

- **"Open the channels"** — a common qigong instruction meaning, in plain
  terms, to relax and align the body so movement and breath feel unimpeded.
- **"Qi sinks to the dantian"** (氣沉丹田) — the instruction to let the breath
  settle into the [lower abdomen](../glossary/dantian.md), which the map
  connects to the body's central channels.
- **The three gates** ([三關, *sān guān*](../glossary/san-guan.md)) — three
  points along the back's *du mai* (Governing Vessel) that internal practice
  traditionally works with.

In practice terms, these are cues about **posture, breath, and attention** —
not instructions to move a literal substance through literal tubes. When a
teacher says "feel the qi in your hands," the useful instruction underneath is
"place your attention in your hands and relax."

---

## What meridians are *not*

Three Western misreadings are worth clearing away.

1. **They are not literal tubes or nerves.** No dissected "meridian" exists.
   The map is conceptual.
2. **They are not scientifically proven energy highways.** The evidence for a
   physical channel system is not established; claims of "proven meridians"
   overstate the research.
3. **They are not required belief.** Qigong and tai chi work as movement,
   breath, and attention training with or without the map.

---

## Who should be cautious

Meridians are a concept, not an exercise, so the ideas here carry little
direct risk. The practical note concerns **acupuncture and acupressure**, which
do involve the body: if you pursue those, use a qualified, licensed
practitioner, and check with a healthcare professional about any health
condition. Nothing on this page is medical advice, and no practice described
here cures or treats disease.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are meridians in one sentence?**
Meridians (經絡, jīngluò) are the network of channels through which Chinese
medicine describes qi — the body's functional energy — as flowing, linking the
organs to the surface of the body.

**Are meridians real anatomical structures?**
No structure matching the meridian map has been found under a microscope. They
are best understood as a functional map — a way of describing how the body's
systems relate — rather than physical tubes or nerves.

**What are the 12 primary meridians?**
The twelve are named for organ systems: lung, large intestine, stomach,
spleen, heart, small intestine, bladder, kidney, pericardium, triple burner,
gallbladder, and liver. Each is paired with a yin or yang quality and linked
in a continuous circuit.

**How are meridians related to acupuncture?**
Acupuncture points (acupoints) are located along the meridian lines.
Acupuncture and acupressure work by stimulating those points, in the
traditional model, to influence the flow of qi through the channels.

**Do I need to believe in meridians to practice qigong or tai chi?**
No. The practices work as movement, breath, and attention training regardless.
Meridians are a traditional map some practitioners find useful; you can
practice fully without adopting it.

**What do meridians feel like?**
Practitioners sometimes describe sensations — warmth, tingling, or a feeling
of "flow" — along the channel lines, but these vary widely and are not
required or graded. Many feel nothing specific.

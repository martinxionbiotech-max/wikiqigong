# Qigong Principles

> **TL;DR** — Every form of qigong, from flowing routines to standing still,
> is built on the same three pillars: **regulation of body, breath, and mind**
> — the "three regulations" (*santiao*, 三調). Qigong is not a specific
> movement; it is the *skill of harmonizing these three*. Master the three
> regulations and any qigong method becomes understandable.

---

Qigong is a big umbrella — hundreds of methods, some moving, some still. But
underneath every one of them is a single, elegant structure. Classical and
modern teachers alike describe qigong as the practice of **three regulations**:

1. **Regulation of body** (*tiao shen*, 調身) — posture and movement
2. **Regulation of breath** (*tiao xi*, 調息) — breathing
3. **Regulation of mind** (*tiao xin*, 調心) — attention

This page explains each pillar, why the *harmony* of the three matters more
than any one of them, and how to practice them. It is the conceptual key to
everything else on this site.

---

## The one idea: harmony over technique

Here is the single most important thing to understand about qigong, and it
saves years of confusion:

> **Qigong is not a set of movements. It is the skill of bringing body,
> breath, and mind into harmony.**

A person doing the world's most famous qigong routine while thinking about
their grocery list is doing choreography, not qigong. A person standing
perfectly still, breathing slowly, with attention resting in the lower belly —
that *is* qigong, even though no movement is involved.

This is why the three regulations matter more than any specific form. The form
is a container. The three regulations are the content.

Their Chinese name is **santiao** (三調, *sāntiáo*), "the three regulations" —
and the key word is the repeated character **tiao** (調):

- **Tiao** (調) — "to regulate, adjust, tune" — the same character used for
  tuning a musical instrument. It means bringing something into its *right,
  natural* state, not forcing it.

So the three regulations are not three *exercises* to grind through. They are
three things you **tune** — the body (調身 *tiao shen*), the breath (調息 *tiao
xi*), and the mind (調心 *tiao xin*) — until they fall into harmony on their
own. The image of tuning an instrument is the whole philosophy: you adjust
until it resonates, you do not wrench it into place.

---

## Regulation of body (調身 *tiao shen*)

**What it is.** The body is placed in a posture that is *aligned*,
*relaxed*, and *stable* — and, when the practice moves, moves slowly,
smoothly, and without strain.

**Why it matters.** The body is the foundation. If posture is tense or
collapsed, breath becomes shallow and the mind cannot settle. A well-regulated
body is the *condition* that makes the other two regulations possible.

The key qualities, in plain terms:

- **Alignment** — the spine is long but natural; head lightly lifted; the body
  stacks comfortably over its base. No force, no military stiffness.
- **Relaxation** — unnecessary tension is released, especially in the
  shoulders, chest, and jaw. The body is soft but not collapsed.
- **Stability** — weight is settled and grounded, whether sitting, standing,
  or lying down.

**How to practice it.** Before doing anything with breath or mind, spend thirty
seconds arranging the body: sit or stand comfortably, lengthen the spine,
release the shoulders on an exhale, and let weight settle into your base. That
thirty seconds *is* regulation of body.

---

## Regulation of breath (調息 *tiao xi*)

**What it is.** Breath becomes slow, smooth, and natural — and, in most
qigong, gradually deepens into the abdomen ("belly" or diaphragmatic
breathing).

**Why it matters.** Breath is the bridge between body and mind. It is the one
bodily function that runs automatically *and* can be deliberately controlled,
which makes it the natural lever for influencing the nervous system. Slow,
smooth breathing *may help* activate the parasympathetic "rest and digest"
response — the same mechanism studied in relaxation and stress-reduction
research.

A few important clarifications:

- **Natural before deep.** Beginners should not force anything. The first step
  is simply *noticing* the breath; the second is letting it slow and deepen on
  its own.
- **Never strain.** Breath-holding, hyperventilation, or forced "power
  breathing" is not beginner qigong and can be uncomfortable or unsafe.
- **Abdomen over chest.** As the breath deepens, the belly softens and expands
  on the inhale, and falls on the exhale — the opposite of the tense,
  shoulders-up chest breathing that comes with stress.

**How to practice it.** Sitting or standing relaxed, place one hand on your
belly. Breathe slowly through the nose, letting the belly rise into your hand
on the inhale and fall away on the exhale. Keep the shoulders still. That is
regulation of breath — and it can be done anywhere, anytime.

## Regulation of mind (調心 *tiao xin*)

**What it is.** Attention is gathered and gently placed — on the breath, on a
sensation, or on a focal point like the [dantian](../glossary/dantian.md) — and
held there with patience rather than force.

**Why it matters.** The mind is the director. Body and breath can be perfectly
regulated, but if attention is scattered, the practice is hollow. This pillar
is what links qigong to meditation and why both are *studied for* their
effects on stress and mind-wandering.

The core skill is deceptively simple and endlessly repeatable:

- **Place attention** on something real (the breath, the belly, the feet).
- **Notice drift** when the mind wanders — and it will, constantly.
- **Return gently**, without self-criticism.

That cycle — place, drift, return — is regulation of mind. It is the same
skill taught in secular mindfulness meditation, expressed through a qigong
lens.

**What it is not.** It is not emptying the mind, forcing a blank state, or
visualizing elaborate "energy" imagery. Quiet attention on something ordinary
is the whole method.

---

## Why the three must work together

Each regulation supports the other two — that is the "harmony" at the heart of
the practice:

| Regulation | Supports the others by… |
|------------|------------------------|
| **Body** | Giving breath room to deepen and the mind a stable, relaxed base |
| **Breath** | Calming the nervous system, which releases the body and steadies the mind |
| **Mind** | Directing attention, which keeps posture alive and breath smooth |

A helpful way to check your own practice: when you notice tension, the body is
unregulated; when the breath is quick or shallow, the breath is unregulated;
when the mind wanders and you chase it, the mind is unregulated. The fix is
never "try harder" — it is *return gently* to the pillar that slipped.

## The three regulations and qi

Where does [qi](../glossary/qi.md) fit into all this? In the traditional framing,
the three regulations are the *method* by which qi is cultivated: a regulated
body lets qi flow, a regulated breath moves qi, and a regulated mind directs
qi.

But notice something important: the three regulations are **complete without
qi**. You can practice all three, reap the relaxation and attention benefits,
and never once think about energy. Qi is the traditional *explanation*; the
three regulations are the *practice*. This is why qigong works for skeptics
and believers alike.

## A five-minute practice of all three

Here is the three regulations in a single seated practice you can do now:

1. **Body.** Sit comfortably, feet flat, spine long but relaxed, hands on
   thighs. Soften the shoulders on an exhale. *(1 minute)*
2. **Breath.** Place a hand on your belly and breathe slowly through the nose,
   belly rising and falling, shoulders still. *(2 minutes)*
3. **Mind.** Rest attention lightly on the lower belly — the
   [dantian](../glossary/dantian.md). When the mind drifts, notice and return.
   *(2 minutes)*

That is qigong: body, breath, and mind, harmonized. Everything else — the
routines, the forms, the lineages — is a variation on this three-part seed.

## Who should be cautious

The three regulations are gentle, but a responsible note:

- If you have respiratory or cardiovascular conditions, check with a
  healthcare professional before breath work.
- If slow breathing causes dizziness or discomfort, ease back to natural
  breathing.
- If you have balance problems, practice seated or near support.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the "three regulations" of qigong?**
They are the regulation of body (posture), breath (breathing), and mind
(attention). Qigong is defined by bringing these three into harmony, not by
any particular movement.

**Do I have to learn special breathing techniques?**
No. Beginners should start with natural, relaxed breathing that gradually
deepens into the abdomen. Most qigong uses slow, easy breathing — never forced
or uncomfortable.

**Is qigong just breathing exercises?**
Breath is one of three pillars, not the whole practice. Qigong is body,
breath, and attention working together. Breathing alone, without posture and
attention, is only one-third of the practice.

**What should I focus on while practicing?**
Attention typically rests on the breath, the sensation of the body, or a focal
point like the lower dantian. The aim is steady, gentle attention — not a
blank or forced mind.

**Can the "three regulations" help with stress?**
Research suggests slow abdominal breathing and focused attention may help
activate the body's relaxation response and reduce mind-wandering. Evidence is
promising but varies in quality.

**How is qigong's method different from tai chi's?**
They share the same foundations, but qigong is typically simpler — single
movements, routines, or stillness — with no martial component, while tai chi
expresses these principles through a long, linked martial form.

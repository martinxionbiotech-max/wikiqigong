# Push Hands: Tai Chi's Partner Practice

> **TL;DR** — Push hands (推手, *tuīshǒu*) is tai chi's **two-person training
> exercise**. Partners keep light arm contact and practice **sensitivity,
> yielding, and rooted balance** — the skills that let the solo form's
> principles be tested against another body. It is a cooperative drill, not a
> fight: the goal is to sense and redirect force, not to overpower. You can
> practice tai chi fully without it, but it deepens the art for those who try
> it.

Tai chi is usually pictured as a solo practice — one person moving slowly
through a form. But the art has a partner side, and it is the part that most
clearly reveals whether the solo practice is working. That partner side is
push hands (推手, *tuīshǒu*). This page explains what it is, what it trains,
and how it connects to everything else.

## What the name means

Push hands is called 推手 (*tuīshǒu*), and the name is refreshingly plain:

- **Tui** (推) — "to push."
- **Shou** (手) — "hand" or "hands."

So 推手 is literally **"pushing hands"** — a description of the exercise's
visible surface. But the name is a little misleading, because the real
training is not in the pushing. It is in what happens *around* the push:
sensing the partner's force, yielding to it, and keeping your own balance
while contact shifts.

---

## The three registers

Like every core practice on this site, push hands is clearest in three
separated layers.

### Historical fact

Push hands developed within tai chi as a safe, controlled way to train the
art's martial sensitivity without full-contact fighting. It is practiced in
all five major tai chi styles (see [Tai Chi Five Styles](tai-chi-five-styles.md)),
and its principles are documented in the classical tai chi writings — most
famously the idea of "using four ounces to deflect a thousand pounds" (四兩撥千斤,
*sì liǎng bō qiān jīn*). The exact historical origin is not firmly documented,
and should be read with the same uncertainty as tai chi's own origins.

### Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, push hands trains a cluster of skills, each with a vivid
Chinese name:

| Skill | Chinese | Pinyin | Plain-English meaning |
|-------|---------|--------|-----------------------|
| Listening | 聽勁 | tīngjìn | Sensing a partner's force and intent through touch |
| Sticking | 黏 | nián | Maintaining light, unbroken contact |
| Following | 隨 | suí | Moving *with* the partner rather than against them |
| Yielding | 化勁 | huàjìn | Neutralizing incoming force by turning and releasing |
| Issuing | 發勁 | fājìn | Releasing force from a rooted, connected body |

These are supported by the four cardinal techniques — *peng* (掤, ward-off),
*lu* (捋, roll back), *ji* (擠, press), and *an* (按, push) — the basic
shapes the hands trace through the exercise.

### Modern explanation

Read without the traditional language, push hands is a **partnered balance
and sensitivity drill**. It trains you to (1) keep your structure and
equilibrium while another person applies shifting force, (2) detect the
direction of that force through touch, and (3) respond by yielding and
turning rather than by bracing. These skills transfer directly to the solo
form's core qualities: [song](../glossary/song.md) (relaxed release) and
[rooting](../glossary/gen.md) (grounded stability).

In this sense push hands is the art's **feedback loop** — a way to check
whether the relaxation and rooting you practice alone actually hold up under
contact.

## How push hands relates to the solo form

The solo form and push hands are two halves of one method:

| Solo form (拳架, quánjià) | Push hands (推手, tuīshǒu) |
|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| Practiced alone | Practiced with a partner |
| Builds posture, relaxation, and movement quality | Tests those qualities under contact |
| Trains the body to move as one connected unit | Trains sensitivity to *another* body |
| Slow and uninterrupted | Slow and responsive |

Neither is complete by itself in the traditional view — the solo form builds
the body, and push hands reveals whether the building is sound.

## A first taste: fixed-step push hands

If you have a partner, here is the gentlest introduction (ideally learned from
a teacher or class):

1. **Face your partner**, both in a comfortable forward stance, and touch the
   backs of your wrists or forearms lightly — contact, not grip.
2. **One partner leads.** The leading partner slowly presses forward with
   light, even force.
3. **The other yields.** Instead of pushing back, the receiving partner
   **turns the waist** and lets the incoming force pass to the side, keeping
   the feet still and the contact light.
4. **Swap roles**, back and forth, moving slowly, like a slow-motion
   conversation through the arms.
5. **Keep it cooperative.** The goal is not to win; it is to stay soft,
   balanced, and connected.

Fixed-step push hands keeps the feet planted, which removes the complication
of footwork and lets you focus purely on sensitivity and yielding.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"Push hands is wrestling or fighting."** It is a cooperative training
   exercise, not a contest. The aim is sensitivity, not victory.
2. **"You win by pushing harder."** The skill is in yielding and redirecting,
   not overpowering — force usually defeats the purpose.
3. **"It is only for advanced martial artists."** Gentle, fixed-step push
   hands is taught to beginners and is widely practiced for its own sake.
4. **"Contact means grabbing."** Contact is light and open — sticking, not
   gripping.

---

## Who should be cautious

- Push hands involves **light physical contact and balance challenges**; if
  you have balance, joint, or back conditions, check with a healthcare
  professional first, and keep the practice very gentle or skip it.
- Practice only with a **cooperative partner** who understands it is training,
  not competition — aggressive pushing is how people get hurt.
- Stop if anything causes pain, sharp discomfort, or unsteadiness.

As always, nothing here is medical advice, and push hands is a training
exercise, not a treatment.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is push hands in one sentence?**
Push hands (推手, tuīshǒu) is tai chi's two-person training exercise in which
partners keep light arm contact and practice sensitivity, yielding, and rooted
balance — testing the solo form's principles against another body.

**Is push hands a form of fighting?**
No. It is a cooperative training exercise, not a contest. The aim is to
develop sensitivity and balance, not to defeat a partner — force is usually a
sign you are doing it wrong.

**Do I need a partner to learn tai chi?**
No. Push hands is a valuable complement, but the solo form is complete on its
own. Many people practice tai chi for years without ever doing push hands.

**Can beginners do push hands?**
Yes. Gentle, fixed-step push hands is taught to beginners and is a safe way to
learn yielding and balance. It is usually learned from a teacher or class.

**What is the goal of push hands?**
To keep your structure and balance while another person applies shifting force
— to sense the direction of that force and respond by yielding and turning
rather than bracing.

**Is push hands safe?**
When practiced cooperatively with a partner and light contact, it is
low-impact. Avoid it (or check with a healthcare professional) if you have
balance, joint, or back conditions that could be aggravated.

# Tuna

**What it is:** Tuna (吐納, *tǔ nà*), literally "exhale and inhale," is one of
the oldest named Chinese breathing methods — the deliberate, slow cycle of
letting the breath out and drawing it back in that sits at the root of
[breathing](../glossary/breathing.md) practice in qigong and tai chi.

## Literal meaning

- **Tu** (吐) — "to spit out," "to exhale," "to release."
- **Na** (納) — "to take in," "to receive," "to inhale."

So 吐納 is literally **"exhale and inhale."**

The imagery transfer lives in the two characters. 吐 pictures the mouth (口,
*kǒu*) releasing something outward — the breath *spoken* out, like spitting
out the stale. 納 pictures silk (糸, the thread radical) being *received and
kept* — the breath gathered in like fine thread drawn back. Notice the order:
the word leads with the **exhale**, the letting-go. That sequence — out before
in — is deliberate and echoes a central teaching that the *release* of the
breath is the doorway into relaxation.

## Historical context

Tuna is genuinely ancient. The term appears in early Daoist and
nourishing-life literature, and its practice is pictured in the famous
Mawangdui silk scrolls of the second century BCE — the same Han-dynasty
archaeological find that shows the earliest known
[Dao Yin](../glossary/dao-yin.md) movement charts. Tuna and daoyin traveled
together: daoyin as the movement-and-stretch half, tuna as the breath half.
Over centuries, this breath-and-movement family of practices fed into what we
now call [qigong](../glossary/qigong.md).

## Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, tuna is understood as the way **qi is moved by the breath**:
the exhale was described as guiding qi downward (the phrase 氣沉丹田, *qì chén
dāntián*, "qi sinks to the dantian"), the inhale as gathering it. Slow, quiet,
deep breathing was held to build and conserve qi; quick, shallow breathing to
scatter it. The practice was a core method of *yang sheng* — nourishing life.

## Modern explanation

Read without metaphysics, tuna is simply **slow, deliberate abdominal
breathing**, with the emphasis on the exhale. That emphasis is well aligned
with what is now known: a long, relaxed exhale *may help* engage the
parasympathetic "rest and digest" response, and slow breathing (roughly six
breaths per minute, though individual ranges vary) *has been studied for* its
effects on heart-rate variability — a marker of the balance between stress and
recovery. No belief in qi is required: tuna is a breath technique, and its
relaxation effects are observable and trainable.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"Tuna is about eating fish."** It is the pinyin of 吐納, "exhale and
   inhale" — unrelated to the English word for the fish, which is pure
   coincidence.
2. **"Tuna is a special, secret breathing trick."** It is the ordinary
   deliberate breath cycle — out, then in — refined through slowness and
   attention.
3. **"You must breathe in first."** The classical emphasis is exhale-first: the
   release opens the cycle.
4. **"It is obsolete, replaced by modern qigong."** The term is old, but the
   exhale-first breath it names is still the backbone of qigong and tai chi
   breathing today.

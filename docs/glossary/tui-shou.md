# Tui Shou (Push Hands)

**What it is:** Push hands (推手, *tuīshǒu*) is tai chi's two-person training
exercise in which partners keep light arm contact and practice sensitivity,
yielding, and rooted balance — the "conversation" that lets the solo form's
principles be tested against another body.

## Literal meaning

**Tui** (推) means "to push." **Shou** (手) means "hand" or "hands." So 推手
is literally **"pushing hands"** — a descriptive name for the exercise's
visible surface. Under the push, however, the real training is in *listening,
sticking, and yielding* rather than in shoving.

## Historical context

Push hands developed within tai chi as a safe, controlled way to train the
art's martial sensitivity without full-contact fighting. It is practiced in
all five major tai chi styles (see
[tai chi styles](../glossary/tai-chi-styles.md)), and its principles are
documented in the classical tai chi writings — most famously the idea of
"using four ounces to deflect a thousand pounds." The exact historical origin
is not firmly documented and should be read with the same uncertainty as tai
chi's own origins.

## Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, push hands trains a cluster of skills named with vivid Chinese
terms: **listening** (聽勁, *tīngjìn*) — sensing a partner's force and intent;
**sticking** (黏, *nián*) — maintaining light, unbroken contact; **following**
(隨, *suí*) — moving with the partner rather than against them; **yielding**
(化勁, *huàjìn*) — neutralizing incoming force by turning and releasing; and
**issuing** (發勁, *fājìn*) — releasing force from a rooted, connected body.
The four cardinal techniques are *peng* (掤, ward-off), *lu* (捋, roll back),
*ji* (擠, press), and *an* (按, push).

## Modern explanation

Read without the traditional language, push hands is a **partnered balance and
sensitivity drill**. It trains you to keep your structure and equilibrium
while another person applies shifting force, to detect the direction of that
force through touch, and to respond by yielding and turning rather than by
bracing. The skills transfer directly to the solo form's core qualities:
[song](../glossary/song.md) (relaxed release) and
[rooting](../glossary/gen.md) (grounded stability).

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"Push hands is wrestling or fighting."** It is a cooperative training
   exercise, not a contest; the aim is sensitivity, not victory.
2. **"You win by pushing harder."** The skill is in yielding and redirecting,
   not in overpowering — force usually defeats the purpose.
3. **"It is only for advanced martial artists."** Gentle, fixed-step push
   hands is taught to beginners and is widely practiced for its own sake.
4. **"Contact means grabbing."** Contact is light and open — sticking, not
   gripping.

# Yi

**What it is:** Yi (意, *yì*), "intention" or "mind," is the middle layer of
tai chi's form–intention–energy progression ([xing, yi,
qi](../glossary/xing-yi-qi.md)) — the mind's *directed focus* that fills the
shape of a movement and, in classical teaching, guides the qi.

## Literal meaning

意 means "intention," "idea," "thought," or "meaning."

The imagery transfer is in the character's make-up. 意 is built from 心
(*xīn*, "heart-mind") beneath 音 (*yīn*, "sound"): the heart-mind attending to
a sound — the mind *leaning toward* something, focused on it. This is exactly
what yi means in practice: not wandering thought, but **attention aimed at an
object** — the deliberate direction of the mind. It is the difference between
hearing noise and *listening*.

## Historical context

Yi is a core term across Chinese internal-arts and meditation literature,
especially from the Ming and Qing periods onward, where the classical sequence
was repeated: first correct the outer form, then invest it with intention, then
let qi follow. The saying 意到氣到 (*yì dào qì dào*), "where intention goes, qi
follows," captures the link between the second and third layers and remains a
touchstone of internal practice. Yi also features in the "internal harmonies"
(內三合, *nèi sān hé*), which pair the mind, intention, and qi with the body's
force.

## Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, yi is the **conductor** of practice. The form is the container;
yi is what fills it; qi is what flows when yi is steady. A movement done
without yi is described as "empty" — a shape with no life in it. With yi, the
same shape becomes "full": the mind is placed *in* the movement, not doing it
on autopilot. This is why teachers say "use intention, not force" — let the
mind direct, and the body and breath follow.

## Modern explanation

Read without metaphysics, yi is **focused attention** — the mind aimed at the
body, the breath, or a direction of movement rather than wandering. Modern
movement science describes the same phenomenon as *attentional focus*: when
attention is placed on the body (an "internal focus") or on the movement's
effect, coordination and quality of movement change measurably. "Where
intention goes, qi follows" becomes, in modern terms, "where attention goes,
the body organizes itself accordingly." No belief in qi is required — yi is
simply practicing with awareness.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"Yi is the same as shen."** Shen is the mind's general *awareness*; yi is
   that awareness *aimed* at something — attention with a direction.
2. **"Yi means thinking hard or visualizing forcefully."** It is gentle,
   directed attention, not straining. Forced concentration creates the tension
   the practice is trying to dissolve.
3. **"You can skip to yi without learning the form."** Intention needs a shape
   to inhabit — the form comes first, then the mind is placed in it.
4. **"Yi requires belief in qi."** The layer works entirely as *attention*;
   the qi language is a traditional way of naming what focused attention feels
   like.

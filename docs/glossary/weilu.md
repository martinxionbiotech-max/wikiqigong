# Weilu

**What it is:** Weilu (尾閭, *wěi lǘ*), the "tail gate," is the lowest of the
spine's three gates in classical internal practice — the **coccyx (tailbone)**
at the base of the spine, where the body's central channel was said to begin
and qi to rise.

## Literal meaning

- **Wei** (尾) — "tail."
- **Lü** (閭) — "gate," "door," "village gate."

So 尾閭 is literally **"tail gate."**

The imagery transfer is in the pairing: 尾 pictures the tailbone (the body's
"tail"), and 閭 pictures a gate — originally the *gate of a village*, an
archway through a wall. Together they name the coccyx as a *gate at the tail*:
the lowest doorway of the spine. In the traditional map, this is where the
upward path of the [microcosmic orbit](../glossary/xiao-zhou-tian.md) begins —
the first pass the breath and attention must move through on the way up.

## Historical context

Weilu is one of the three gates (see
[San Guan](../glossary/san-guan.md)) of Daoist inner-alchemy (*neidan*) and its
descendants — the map of three narrow points along the spine: weilu (the
tailbone), jiaji (between the shoulder blades), and yuzhen (the base of the
skull). The terms appear in internal-cultivation literature over centuries,
with the precise systematization varying by school; the map is a teaching
device, not a medical anatomy, and individual text dating is often uncertain.

## Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, weilu was understood as the *first gate* — the place where
rising qi, drawn up from the lower
[dantian](../glossary/dantian.md), could be obstructed. A tucked but relaxed
tailbone was described as "opening" the gate, allowing qi to pass upward along
the spine. The instruction to "drop the tailbone" (尾閭下沉, *wěilǘ xiàchén*) —
gently tucking the coccyx under — became a standard posture cue precisely to
keep this lowest gate open.

## Modern explanation

Read without metaphysics, weilu is simply the **coccyx and its alignment**.
"Dropping the tailbone" is a real and useful postural cue: a gently tucked,
relaxed coccyx levels the pelvis and lengthens the lower spine — the same
neutral-pelvis idea modern posture and movement teaching emphasizes. This
supports grounded stance ([rooting](../glossary/gen.md)), lets the hips
([kua](../glossary/kua.md)) settle, and takes strain off the lower back. The
"gate" is a poetic name for a real anatomical region where habit and tension
commonly concentrate.

## Related concepts

- **[San Guan (The Three Gates)](../glossary/san-guan.md)** — the three spine gates, of which weilu is the lowest.
- **[Dantian](../glossary/dantian.md)** — the lower center from which qi was said to rise through weilu.
- **[Xiao Zhou Tian (Microcosmic Orbit)](../glossary/xiao-zhou-tian.md)** — the circuit that passes up through the tailbone gate.
- **[Kua](../glossary/kua.md)** — the hips, which settle together with the tailbone.
- **[Rooting](../glossary/gen.md)** — grounded stance, supported by a tucked, relaxed coccyx.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"Weilu is a physical opening in the tailbone."** It is a *map* of a
   transition zone — the coccyx and its alignment — not an anatomical valve.
2. **"You must force qi up through it."** Traditional teaching emphasizes
   release and a relaxed tuck, not effort; forcing recreates the tension that
   "closes" the gate.
3. **"Tucking the tailbone means clenching the buttocks."** The cue is a gentle,
   relaxed tuck — clenching creates rigidity, the opposite of the goal.
4. **"It only matters in esoteric practice."** As a posture cue for a level
   pelvis and long lower spine, it matters in ordinary standing and movement
   for everyone.

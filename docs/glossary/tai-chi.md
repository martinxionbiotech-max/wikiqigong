# Tai Chi

**What it is:** Tai chi (太極拳, *tàijíquán*) is a Chinese martial art whose
movements are practiced slowly and continuously, making it — almost by
accident — one of the most effective health and balance exercises ever devised.

## Literal meaning

**Taiji** (太極) is the "Supreme Ultimate" — the undivided whole from which
[yin and yang](../glossary/yin-yang.md) emerge. **Quan** (拳) means "fist," and
by extension a boxing or fighting art. So the name literally names a *fighting
art built on the principle of balance*. The Western shortening to "tai chi"
(dropping *quan*) hides its martial origin.

## Historical context

The legend credits the Daoist Zhang Sanfeng, but this is founding myth, not
history. The earliest well-documented tai chi is **Chen-style**, associated
with the Chen family of Chenjiagou in Henan province (17th century). **Yang
Luchan** adapted it in the 19th century into the flowing, even-paced style most
people picture today. From these grew the Wu, Wu/Hao, and Sun styles — five
major family lineages in total.

## Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, tai chi trains the body to move with [qi](../glossary/qi.md) —
relaxed, connected, and rooted — so that force is absorbed and redirected
rather than met head-on. The slow pace is deliberate: it lets the practitioner
feel and refine the internal quality of each movement.

## Modern explanation

Tai chi is, mechanically, an excellent balance and mobility exercise: slow
weight shifts train dynamic balance, a low center of gravity strengthens legs
and core, and inward attention *may help* relaxation. Its strongest evidence
is in **fall prevention in older adults**.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"Tai chi is a dance."** It is a martial art performed slowly.
2. **"Tai chi is only for old people."** It is practiced at every age.
3. **"Tai chi is a religion."** It has philosophical roots but requires no
   belief system.
4. **"Tai chi cures disease."** It *may help* balance and relaxation; it does
   not cure.

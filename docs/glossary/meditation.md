# Meditation

**What it is:** Meditation (靜坐, *jìngzuò*, "quiet sitting") is the practice
of steadying attention — training the mind to rest on one thing (often the
breath) and to return gently when it wanders.

## Literal meaning

The most common classical Chinese term is 靜坐 (*jìngzuò*): 靜 (*jìng*),
"quiet, still, calm," and 坐 (*zuò*), "to sit." The name is descriptive, not
mystical — it names exactly what the practice looks like: sitting quietly and
cultivating stillness. Another common term is 冥想 (*míngxiǎng*), "deep
thought," closer to the English "contemplation." The older, more widely used
term in self-cultivation traditions is *jìngzuò*.

## Historical context

Meditation in China developed along two main streams that eventually merged:

- **Daoist quiet sitting** — part of internal alchemy, aiming to still the
  body and mind so that vitality could gather and refine. The
  [Shen](shen.md) (spirit/mind) was to be "settled" through stillness.
- **Buddhist meditation** — arriving from India around the early centuries CE,
  bringing techniques of breath-following and attention training (*chan*, the
  ancestor of Zen).

These traditions cross-pollinated for over a millennium. The result is that
Chinese meditation — and, through it, tai chi and qigong — treats stillness of
mind not as an escape from the body but as something rooted *in* the body: in
the breath, in posture, in the [dantian](dantian.md).

## Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, meditation is the practice of "settling the
[shen](shen.md)" — calming the mind's aware, luminous quality so that
it rests within rather than scattering outward. The body is arranged (upright
but relaxed), the breath is allowed to slow, and attention is placed on the
breath or the dantian. Distraction is not a failure but the raw material of
practice: the traditional image is of a muddy pool that becomes clear only
when it is left still.

## Modern explanation

Meditation is the most thoroughly studied of all mind–body practices, and the
modern framing is well-established:

- **Attention training** — meditation *has been studied for* its effect on
  mind-wandering, showing that repeated "returning" strengthens the ability to
  sustain attention.
- **Stress response** — regular practice *may help* reduce self-reported
  stress and anxiety, with evidence generally considered stronger than for
  many other mind–body techniques, though effect sizes vary.
- **Mechanism** — much of the benefit is understood through the same slow
  breathing and relaxation pathways described in the
  [Breathing](breathing.md) entry.

As always: meditation is a practice, not a treatment. It *may support*
relaxation and attention; it does not cure disease, and nothing here is
medical advice.

## Related concepts

- **[Shen](shen.md)** — meditation is the primary practice for
  settling the shen.
- **[Breathing](breathing.md)** — the breath is the most common
  object of meditation attention.
- **[Tai Chi](tai-chi.md)** — tai chi is described as "meditation in
  motion," applying meditative attention to movement.
- **[Qigong](qigong.md)** — seated qigong is itself a form of
  meditation.
- **[Dantian](dantian.md)** — attention in meditation often rests on
  the lower dantian.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"Meditation means emptying the mind of all thought."** The aim is
   steadier attention, not blankness. Thoughts arise; the practice is noticing
   and returning, not suppressing.
2. **"You must sit in lotus posture."** Chinese tradition is flexible —
   sitting in a chair is perfectly acceptable, and comfort matters more than
   posture purity.
3. **"Meditation is religious."** It has religious roots, but the attention
   training it teaches is entirely secular and well-studied. No belief is
   required.
4. **"If I'm distracted, I'm doing it wrong."** Distraction *is* the practice
   — every return is a repetition that builds the skill.

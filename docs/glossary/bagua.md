# Bagua

**What it is:** Bagua (八卦, *bā guà*), the "eight trigrams," are the eight
figures made by stacking three lines, each line broken (yin) or unbroken
(yang) — a compact binary grammar that encodes the whole
[yin–yang](../glossary/yin-yang.md) system and underlies much of Chinese
cosmology and internal practice.

## Literal meaning

- **Ba** (八) — "eight."
- **Gua** (卦) — "trigram," a divination figure built from lines.

So 八卦 is literally **"eight trigrams"** — or "eight symbols."

The imagery transfer is in 卦, which grew out of yarrow-stalk divination: the
word pictures the *results* of a casting, recorded as a column of horizontal
lines. A **broken line** (⚋) is yin; an **unbroken line** (⚰) is yang. Stack
three of these and you get 2 × 2 × 2 = **eight** possible figures. The trigrams
are thus not eight arbitrary symbols — they are the *exhaustive* combinations
of yin and yang in three positions, a picture of the binary logic behind the
whole system.

## Historical context

The trigrams are documented in the *Book of Changes* (*Yijing*, 易經), one of
the oldest Chinese classics, whose core layers date from the early first
millennium BCE (with later commentary layers added over centuries). In the
*Yijing*, the eight trigrams are paired into the 64 hexagrams used for
divination and, more importantly, for reasoning about change. The eight
trigrams became a general cosmological map — matched to directions, seasons,
family roles, and the body — and were later absorbed into Daoist and
internal-arts teaching.

## Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, the eight trigrams name the fundamental states of change:
Heaven (乾 *qián*), Earth (坤 *kūn*), Thunder (震 *zhèn*), Wind (巽 *xùn*),
Water (坎 *kǎn*), Fire (離 *lí*), Mountain (艮 *gèn*), and Lake (兌 *duì*).
In tai chi's classical vocabulary, the **eight methods** (*bāfǎ*, 八法) — peng,
lu, ji, an, cai, lie, zhou, kao — were read against the eight trigrams, and the
**five steps** against the five phases (see
[The Eight Methods & Five Steps](../articles/eight-methods.md)). This is a
*mnemonic correspondence*, a way of saying the art's technical system echoes the
same yin–yang logic as the cosmos.

## Modern explanation

Read without the traditional frame, the trigrams are an early **combinatorial
notation** — a way of writing down states built from two opposites. That is
genuinely elegant: with two marks and three positions you can name eight
states, and by pairing them, sixty-four. Its modern value is conceptual, not
supernatural — a reminder that complex systems are often built from simple
oppositions in combination. The same "eight methods / five steps" mapping can
be read as pure structure: a taxonomy of movement directions and force
qualities, no cosmology required.

## Related concepts

- **[Yin–Yang](../glossary/yin-yang.md)** — the two line-states the trigrams combine.
- **[Wu Xing](../glossary/wu-xing.md)** — the five phases, the other classical map of change.
- **[Tai Chi](../glossary/tai-chi.md)** — the art whose eight methods echo the eight trigrams.
- **[Neigong](../glossary/neigong.md)** — internal practice that borrows the trigram map as a frame.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"Bagua is fortune-telling."** The trigrams originate in divination, but
   they function as a *reasoning system about change* — and in tai chi, as a
   structural map — not a prediction tool.
2. **"Bagua only means the martial art Baguazhang."** Baguazhang (八卦掌,
   "Eight Trigrams Palm") is a *martial art* that borrows the name; 八卦 itself
   is the older cosmological scheme of the eight trigrams.
3. **"The eight trigrams are eight literal objects."** They are *states* —
   combinations of yin and yang — not eight physical things.
4. **"Tai chi secretly depends on the trigrams."** The trigram mapping is a
   classical mnemonic; the movements stand on their own as a movement taxonomy
   without any cosmological layer.

# Yi Jin Jing

**What it is:** Yi Jin Jing (易筋經, *yì jīn jīng*), the "Muscle/Tendon Change
Classic," is a traditional Chinese stretching qigong that uses slow, sustained
stretches and coordinated breathing to lengthen and strengthen the tendons,
ligaments, and connective tissue (fascia).

## Literal meaning

**Yi** (易) means "change," "transform," or "exchange." **Jin** (筋) means
"sinews" — the tendons, ligaments, and connective tissue that bind muscle to
bone. **Jing** (經) means "classic" or "canonical text." So the name is
literally "the classic of changing the sinews."

## Historical context

The name *Yijinjing* first appears in written records from the 17th century,
with the earliest known manuscript dating to the Ming–Qing transition (the
1600s). Tradition attributes the text to Bodhidharma (達摩, *Dámó*) or the
general Yue Fei, but there is no reliable historical evidence for either —
the Bodhidharma attribution is a Shaolin legend. A standardized modern
version was published by China's health authorities in the early 2000s.

## Traditional interpretation

In the traditional frame, the *jin* (sinews) are understood as a distinct
layer of the body — the channels through which strength is transmitted and,
in some teachings, through which [qi](../glossary/qi.md) is said to move. The
practice "changes" the sinews: gradually lengthening and supple-ing them so
strength flows without obstruction. It was taught as an internal
strengthening practice complementing external martial conditioning.

## Modern explanation

Read in modern terms, Yi Jin Jing is a **stretching and mobility practice
with an emphasis on connective tissue.** Slow, sustained stretching — held
for a few breaths — *is studied for* its effects on flexibility and on the
health of tendons and fascia. The coordinated breath is a form of attention
training, and the slow loaded holds build functional strength in the tendons
and the stabilizing muscles around the joints.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"It is a secret martial-art method."** Its origins are tied to Shaolin
   legend, but the modern practice is a health and stretching routine open to
   anyone.
2. **"Bodhidharma invented it."** This is legend, not history. The surviving
   texts are 17th-century or later, with no reliable evidence linking them to
   Bodhidharma.
3. **"You must stretch hard to progress."** The opposite — the method works
   through sustained, gentle length, never pain.
4. **"It only works the muscles."** Its defining emphasis is the connective
   tissue — tendons, ligaments, and fascia — which adapt slowly and reward
   consistent practice.

# San Guan (The Three Gates)

**What it is:** San Guan (三關, *sān guān*), "the three gates," is the
traditional map of three narrow points along the spine — the tailbone, the
upper back, and the base of the skull — through which the internal practice of
tai chi and qigong was said to guide attention and qi.

## Literal meaning

**San** (三) means "three"; **guan** (關) means "gate," "pass," or "barrier."
The name pictures the spine as a road with three checkpoints — places where
the classical tradition said the flow of qi could slow or stick.

## Historical context

The three gates come from Daoist inner-alchemy (*neidan*) and its
descendants, where they describe the path along the spine that internal
practice was said to trace. The gates are, from bottom to top:

| Gate | Chinese | Pinyin | Location |
|------|---------|--------|----------|
| **Weilu** | 尾閭 | wěilǘ | the tailbone / coccyx |
| **Jiaji** | 夾脊 | jiājǐ | between the shoulder blades |
| **Yuzhen** | 玉枕 | yùzhěn | the base of the skull ("jade pillow") |

These terms appear in internal-cultivation literature over centuries; the
precise systematization varies by school, and the dating of individual texts
is often uncertain. The map is a teaching device, not a medical anatomy.

## Traditional interpretation

Traditionally, the three gates were understood as the places where rising qi
could be obstructed. Practice — relaxed posture, slow breathing, and gathered
attention — was described as "opening" or "passing" the gates one by one,
allowing qi to rise from the [dantian](../glossary/dantian.md) up the spine. A
straight but relaxed spine was seen as the condition that kept the gates
open.

## Modern explanation

Read without metaphysics, the three gates are a useful map of the spine's
three natural transition zones — the sacrum, the thoracic curve between the
shoulder blades, and the junction of neck and skull. These are exactly the
places where habitual tension and collapsed posture concentrate. "Opening the
gates" is, in modern terms, simply releasing that tension and restoring a
long, aligned, relaxed spine — which supports breathing, balance, and body
awareness, all ordinary and trainable.

## Related concepts

- **[Qi](../glossary/qi.md)** — what the gates were said to let through.
- **[Dantian](../glossary/dantian.md)** — the lower abdomen from which the
  upward path begins.
- **[Neigong](../glossary/neigong.md)** — the internal work that uses this map.
- **[Breathing](../glossary/breathing.md)** — the attention rides the breath
  along the spine.
- **[Tai Chi](../glossary/tai-chi.md)** — whose aligned posture keeps the
  gates open.

## Common misunderstandings

1. **"The gates are literal anatomical valves."** They are a map of the
   spine's transition zones, not physical openings you can surgically locate.
2. **"You must force qi up through them."** Traditional teaching emphasizes
   release and relaxation, not effort; forcing recreates the tension that
   closes the gates.
3. **"The gates are a secret or supernatural."** They are a plain teaching
   device for posture and attention, widely documented across internal
   traditions.

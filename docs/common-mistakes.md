# Common Mistakes in Tai Chi and Qigong (and How to Fix Them)

> **TL;DR** — Almost every beginner mistake in tai chi and qigong traces back
> to **trying too hard**: tensing muscles, forcing the breath, straining for a
> "correct" posture. The fixes are gentle ones — **soften, breathe naturally,
> sink the weight, keep knees over toes, and practice a little every day**
> rather than a lot occasionally.

Every beginner makes mistakes. That is not failure — it is how the body
learns. But some habits, left uncorrected, quietly block progress or even
cause discomfort. This page names the most common ones and, more importantly,
how to fix each.

---

## The mistakes at a glance

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
|---------|--------------------|---------|
| **Over-effort** | Tension, strain, forcing | Soften — song (release) is the skill |
| **Forced breathing** | Deep, loud, or held breath | Breathe naturally, lengthen the exhale gently |
| **Collapsing knees** | Knee drifts inward past the toe | Track the knee over the foot; lower the stance |
| **Skipping the root** | Movement with no connection to the ground | Sink the weight; practice standing first |
| **Rushing the form** | Fast, disconnected movements | Slow down; move continuously and evenly |
| **All-or-nothing practice** | Long, infrequent sessions | Short, daily practice beats occasional marathons |

---

## Mistake 1 — Over-effort (trying too hard)

The most common mistake of all, and the root of most others.

- **What it looks like:** raised shoulders, clenched jaw, locked joints,
  forcing postures deeper than the body wants.
- **Why it happens:** beginners treat tai chi like strength training, where
  effort equals progress. Here the opposite is true.
- **The fix:** the foundational quality is [song](articles/song.md)
  (鬆, relaxed release) — soft, open, tension-free structure. Scan your body
  before each session and deliberately release the shoulders, jaw, and hands.
  If you are working hard, you are almost certainly working wrong.

---

## Mistake 2 — Forced breathing

- **What it looks like:** deep, exaggerated breaths; holding the breath;
  breathing loudly or through gritted teeth.
- **Why it happens:** a misunderstanding that "deep breathing" means "hard
  breathing."
- **The fix:** breathe through the nose, naturally and quietly. Let the belly
  soften on the inhale and the exhale lengthen on its own — you are *allowing*
  the breath to slow, not forcing it. Read [Qigong Breathing](articles/qigong-breathing.md).
  If you feel dizzy, you are over-breathing; ease off.

---

## Mistake 3 — Collapsing knees

The most important alignment error, because it can cause discomfort over time.

- **What it looks like:** during a stance or weight shift, the knee drifts
  inward toward or past the big toe instead of tracking over the foot.
- **The fix:** keep the knee aligned with the toes — the knee travels in the
  direction the foot points. Lower the depth of your stance until the
  alignment holds; depth comes later, alignment comes first. Read
  [Rooting](articles/rooting.md) for stance mechanics.

---

## Mistake 4 — Skipping the root

- **What it looks like:** movement that floats — weight never settles, the
  body bobs, balance relies on tension rather than structure.
- **Why it happens:** beginners want to "do the movements" before learning to
  stand.
- **The fix:** spend time in [Zhan Zhuang](articles/zhan-zhuang.md)
  (standing like a post) before and alongside the moving forms. Sink the
  weight through the feet, soften the knees, and let the ground support you.
  The moving form grows out of a stable root.

---

## Mistake 5 — Rushing the form

- **What it looks like:** performing the sequence quickly, movements
  disconnected, attention ahead of the body.
- **The fix:** slow down. Move **continuously and evenly**, as if through a
  gentle resistance, with no pause and no hurry. The slowness is not a style
  choice — it is how the nervous system learns the coordination. One slow,
  correct repetition is worth ten rushed ones.

---

## Mistake 6 — All-or-nothing practice

- **What it looks like:** an intense two-hour session once a week, then
  nothing; or giving up because "I don't have an hour."
- **The fix:** consistency beats length. **10–20 minutes most days** is far
  more effective than occasional marathons. Even five minutes of standing or
  slow breathing, done daily, builds the habit and the baseline. See the
  [Learning Path](learning-path.md).

---

## A quick self-check before each session

Run through these three questions in the first minute of practice:

1. **Are my shoulders and jaw soft?** If not, release them.
2. **Is my breath quiet and through the nose?** If not, ease it back to
   natural.
3. **Is my weight sinking through my feet?** If not, settle into the stance.

This ten-second check catches most of the mistakes above before they take
hold.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the single most common mistake beginners make?**
Trying too hard — tensing muscles, forcing deep breaths, and straining for a
"correct" posture. The foundational quality of tai chi and qigong is song
(relaxed release), and almost every other error traces back to over-effort
rather than under-effort.

**Is it normal to feel tense or awkward at first?**
Yes. It takes time for slow, soft, coordinated movement to feel natural.
Tension and awkwardness in the first weeks are normal and expected — they are
not a sign that you are doing it wrong.

**How do I know if my knees are collapsing?**
If, during a weight shift or stance, your knee drifts inward toward or past
your big toe instead of tracking over your foot, it is collapsing. The fix is
to keep the knee aligned with the toes and lower the stance depth until the
alignment holds.

**Should I breathe in through the nose and out through the mouth?**
Nose breathing for both in and out is the general default, with a slow,
relaxed exhale. Some methods (like Liu Zi Jue) add a soft voiced exhale, but
for ordinary practice, breathe through the nose and never force the breath.

**Am I practicing too much?**
If you feel sharp pain, exhaustion, or dread about practicing, yes — back off.
Daily 10–20 minute sessions beat long, draining ones. Rest and recovery are
part of the practice.

**Can I fix these habits on my own?**
Most of them, yes — with careful attention and honest self-observation. The
ones involving alignment (knees, posture) benefit most from an occasional
teacher's eye, in person or through a good video.

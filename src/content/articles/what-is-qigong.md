# What Is Qigong?

> **TL;DR** — Qigong (氣功, *qìgōng*) means "qi work" or "breath work."
> It is a Chinese system of gentle movement, breathing, and focused attention
> practiced for health, relaxation, and balance. The core idea is simple:
> **when breath, movement, and attention work together, the body and mind
> settle.** You do not need to believe in qi to practice it. It is not a
> religion, not a martial art, and not a cure for disease.

Qigong (氣功, *qìgōng*) is a Chinese system of gentle movement, breathing,
and focused attention practiced for health, relaxation, and balance. The name
means, very literally, "qi work" — the cultivation of the body's functional
energy through breath and intention.

But that one-line definition hides a much more interesting story. Qigong is
not one exercise. It is an umbrella word for hundreds of practices, some
standing still, some flowing, some lying down, that all share a single simple
idea: **when breath, movement, and attention work together, the body and mind
settle.** This page explains what qigong actually is, where it came from, how
it works, and how a complete beginner can start — without any prior knowledge
of Chinese culture or language.

---

## What the word actually means

Let us slow down on the name, because it carries the whole philosophy.

**Qi** (氣) is the concept of the body's functional energy — breath, warmth,
circulation, and vitality considered as one interconnected system. Its root
meaning is "vapor" or "breath," the steam rising from cooking rice. (For a
full treatment, see our [Qi](/glossary/qi/) entry.)

**Gong** (功) means "work" or "skill acquired through practice" — the same
*gong* as in *gongfu* (功夫, "kung fu"), which simply means skill developed
over time.

So **qigong = "qi work"** or **"breath work"**: the deliberate, repeated
practice of moving and breathing in ways that, by tradition, build and
circulate qi. Notice what the name is *not* saying. It is not "qi magic." It
is not "energy healing." It is **work** — the everyday, patient kind.

> A useful parallel: if tai chi is "meditation in motion," qigong is "breath
> in motion." Tai chi is a martial art with a meditative method; qigong is a
> health practice that can be fully stationary. They overlap heavily but are
> not the same thing. (See [Tai Chi vs Qigong](/articles/tai-chi-vs-qigong/)
> for the full comparison.)

## A short, honest history

Qigong as a *named, unified practice* is younger than most people assume. The
term "qigong" in its modern health sense was popularized in the **1950s**, when
Chinese physicians and martial-arts teachers began standardizing older
breathing and movement traditions under one banner for public health. So when
you hear "ancient qigong," it is worth holding two truths at once:

1. The **ingredients** of qigong are genuinely ancient — breathing techniques,
   slow movement, and seated meditation appear in Chinese sources going back
   over two thousand years.
2. The **label and the modern package** are recent — a mid-20th-century
   synthesis, not a single unbroken lineage.

Where do the ingredients come from?

- **Daoist inner alchemy (*neidan*)** — the tradition that reinterpreted
  alchemy as internal work with breath and attention, ancestor of the dantian
  and the three treasures ([Jing](/glossary/jing/), [Qi](/glossary/qi/),
  [Shen](/glossary/shen/)).
- **Chinese medicine** — the *Huangdi Neijing* (compiled around the Han
  dynasty) describes health as the smooth flow of qi, and movement and breath
  as ways to maintain it.
- **Martial arts** — many martial traditions (including tai chi's ancestors)
  developed internal conditioning methods to build stability and awareness.
- **Buddhist meditation** — which contributed techniques of stilling the mind.

Modern qigong blends these into simple, health-focused routines that require
no religious belief and no martial training. The most widely practiced set in
the world today, **Baduanjin** ("Eight Pieces of Brocade"), was standardized in
this modern period precisely so that anyone could learn it.

## The four traditional categories of qigong

Because "qigong" is an umbrella term, it helps to know the four classic
families a given practice may belong to. Most people begin with the first two.

| Category | Chinese | Purpose | Typical form | Example |
|----------|---------|---------|--------------|---------|
| **Health / medical qigong** | 醫療氣功 *yīliáo qìgōng* | Maintain health, support recovery alongside medical care | Gentle movement + breathing routines | [Baduanjin](/articles/baduanjin/), Liu Zi Jue |
| **Martial qigong** | 武術氣功 *wǔshù qìgōng* | Build stability, power, and body awareness for fighting arts | Standing postures, conditioned breathing | [Zhan Zhuang](/articles/zhan-zhuang/), Iron Shirt |
| **Scholarly / meditative qigong** | 儒家氣功 *rújiā qìgōng* | Cultivate calm, focus, and moral character | Seated meditation + quiet breathing | Confucian "quiet sitting" |
| **Spiritual / Daoist qigong** | 道家氣功 *dàojiā qìgōng* | Inner transformation and longevity practice | Internal alchemy, visualization | Neidan (inner alchemy) |

The modern, secular health practice almost always draws from the **health**
and **martial** families. You will rarely encounter the spiritual traditions
outside dedicated schools, and they are not required for any health benefit.

## What qigong actually involves

Strip away the history and the terminology, and qigong comes down to three
ingredients, always present, always together:

### 1. Movement (or stillness)

Some qigong flows through slow, continuous movements. Some — like **Zhan
Zhuang** — is completely still, holding a standing posture for minutes at a
time. Some is done lying down. The common thread is that the body is *soft*,
*aligned*, and *relaxed* rather than forced. There is no strain, no "no pain
no gain."

### 2. Breathing

Breath is the engine of qigong. Most practice uses slow, natural breathing
that descends into the abdomen — what is often called "diaphragmatic" or
"belly" breathing — coordinated with movement. The breath is never forced or
held uncomfortably.

### 3. Attention

The mind is not empty; it is *placed*. Attention rests on the breath, on the
sensation of the body, or on a focal point like the [dantian](/glossary/dantian/)
— the lower abdomen. This is what separates qigong from ordinary stretching:
the *quality of attention* you bring to it.

When these three combine, practitioners describe a characteristic state:
relaxed but awake, warm, settled, and quietly focused. You do not have to
believe in qi to experience it. The sensations — warmth, tingling, calm — are
the same whether you call them "qi" or "the relaxation response."

## How qigong is believed to work

Two honest framings, both true, neither requiring belief in the other.

**The traditional framing.** Health is the smooth, balanced flow of qi.
Practicing qigong builds qi, moves it through the body's channels, and clears
blockage. Illness is understood as stagnation or deficiency; practice restores
flow. This is a *model* — a way of talking about the body that has guided
practice for centuries.

**The modern framing.** What qigong demonstrably does is combine slow movement,
deep breathing, and focused attention — three interventions that modern
research has *studied for* their effects on stress and balance:

- **Slow abdominal breathing** *may help* activate the parasympathetic nervous
  system, the body's "rest and digest" state, which *may help* lower arousal
  and support relaxation.
- **Gentle, repeated movement** *may help* improve balance, flexibility, and
  ease of movement — important at every age, and especially studied in older
  adults.
- **Focused attention** *may help* reduce mind-wandering and *may help* with
  stress, in ways similar to other forms of meditation.

### What the research says

The evidence base is real but uneven — some findings are strong, others are
preliminary. The most consistent findings come from studies of **tai chi**
(which shares qigong's core ingredients) in **older adults**, where a
well-known randomized trial and subsequent reviews found that regular practice
*was associated with* fewer falls and better balance (Li et al., 2005). A
comprehensive review of qigong and tai chi research noted evidence *of
possible benefit* for balance, psychological well-being, and some
cardiovascular and respiratory markers, while cautioning that study quality
varies (Jahnke et al., 2010). A systematic review of qigong specifically
reported *possible* reductions in anxiety and depression symptoms, while
noting that study quality varied (Wang et al., 2013). Independent sources such as the U.S. National Center for
Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) and Harvard Health summarize a
similar picture: promising, generally safe, but not a replacement for medical
care.

The key word throughout is *may*. Qigong is well-studied and generally safe,
but the evidence for specific health effects varies in quality. It is a
*practice*, not a treatment. **Qigong does not cure disease, and no claim here
should be read as medical advice.** Its most reliable, honest benefit is
simpler: it teaches people to relax, breathe, and move with awareness — and
those are real skills.

## What qigong is *not*

Clearing away common misreadings is part of accurate teaching.

**It is not a religion.** Qigong has Daoist and Buddhist roots, but the modern
health practice requires no belief system. Atheists and skeptics practice it
comfortably.

**It is not "energy healing."** Traditional language about qi can sound like a
claim to manipulate a literal energy field. Treat the qi language as a useful
model for function, not a measurable force. You are not transmitting anything
to anyone.

**It is not a martial art.** Some qigong descended from martial conditioning,
but practicing it will not teach you to fight. (Tai chi, by contrast, *is* a
martial art practiced slowly.)

**It is not a cure.** The health wording matters. Qigong *may support*
relaxation and balance. It does not *cure*, *treat*, or *guarantee* outcomes.

**It is not about contorting your body.** There are no extreme poses, no
forced flexibility. If it hurts or strains, you are doing something wrong —
that is a feature of the design, not a failure of effort.

## A beginner's first session

Here is a complete, safe, five-minute introduction you can do right now,
sitting in a chair. (Standing versions and full routines are covered in the
practice pages.)

1. **Sit comfortably.** Feet flat, back upright but not rigid, hands resting
   on your thighs. Close your eyes or soften your gaze downward.
2. **Notice your breath.** Do not change it yet. Just feel it, for ten slow
   breaths, at the tip of your nose or in your belly.
3. **Let the breath sink.** On the next breath, let your belly soften and
   expand on the inhale, and fall on the exhale. Keep it slow and easy — no
   forcing.
4. **Add attention.** Rest your mind lightly on the lower abdomen, a few
   finger-widths below the navel — the [dantian](/glossary/dantian/). Every
   time the mind drifts (and it will), gently return it. Drifting is normal;
   returning is the practice.
5. **Stay for five minutes.** Then open your eyes slowly and notice how you
   feel before standing up.

That is qigong. Movement, breath, and attention, together, gently. Everything
else — the forms, the routines, the traditions — is elaboration on this seed.

## Who should be cautious

Qigong is gentle, but a responsible guide notes its limits:

- **If you have a medical condition** — especially cardiovascular, respiratory,
  or balance-related — talk to a healthcare professional before starting any
  new movement practice.
- **If you are pregnant** — gentle qigong is often considered fine, but consult
  your care provider, and avoid any breath-holding or inverted postures.
- **If you have a history of dizziness or fainting** — begin seated, rise
  slowly, and keep sessions short until you know how your body responds.
- **If you have a mental-health condition** — meditative breathing is usually
  supportive, but it is not a substitute for professional care, and some
  people find intense breath focus uncomfortable. Go gently.

A useful general rule: if a practice causes pain, breathlessness, dizziness,
or distress, stop and reconsider. Comfort is not weakness in qigong — it is
the point.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to believe in qi to practice qigong?**
No. Qi is a model, not a requirement. Many practitioners describe the same
sensations (warmth, calm, tingling) in purely physiological terms.

**Is qigong safe?**
For most people, yes — it is gentle and low-impact. As with any new physical
practice, if you have a medical condition, check with a healthcare
professional first. Stop if anything hurts.

**How is qigong different from yoga?**
Both combine movement, breath, and attention, but they come from different
traditions and feel different. Qigong tends to be more continuous and less
posture-focused. See [Qigong vs Yoga](/articles/qigong-vs-yoga/) for details.

**How long before I feel something?**
Sensations vary enormously. Some feel warmth and calm in the first session;
others practice for months before noticing much. Neither is a grade. The
measurable outcome is the *doing*, not a special feeling.

**Can qigong replace medical treatment?**
No. Qigong is a wellness practice, not medicine. It may complement — never
replace — professional medical care.

**Do I need a teacher?**
A teacher is genuinely helpful for correcting posture and understanding the
tradition, and is recommended when available. But the basics above are safe to
explore on your own, and many begin that way.
